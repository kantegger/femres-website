Verónica Villalvazo, also known through her work as Frida Guerrera, describes documenting feminicides in Mexico and maintaining public attention on women whom news cycles and institutions quickly abandon. Naming, verifying, and telling these stories becomes a counter-archive against the bureaucratic conversion of people into case numbers.

The work is inseparable from families. Relatives often become searchers, investigators, legal advocates, and custodians of memory because authorities delay, lose evidence, repeat stereotypes, or refuse to recognize gendered patterns. Villalvazo’s practice shows how journalism can move from extraction toward accompaniment, although that proximity brings emotional cost and serious questions about consent, accuracy, and the circulation of traumatic detail.

The talk is not a substitute for structural research on feminicide, but it makes information labor visible. Digital platforms can preserve names and connect cases; they can also reward sensationalism and expose families to abuse. Feminist documentation therefore requires more than visibility. It requires methods that protect dignity, support collective demands, and turn attention into sustained accountability.
