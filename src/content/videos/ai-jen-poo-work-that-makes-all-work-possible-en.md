In this TEDWomen talk, Ai-jen Poo makes domestic work visible as social infrastructure. Nannies, house cleaners, and home-care workers sustain children, older adults, disabled people, households, and the paid labor of everyone who relies on them. Yet because this work is associated with women, migration, race, and the supposedly private home, it is routinely described as “help” rather than skilled labor. Poo’s central phrase—the work that makes all other work possible—reverses that hierarchy and asks listeners to see care as a foundation of the economy.

The talk joins recognition to organizing. Poo draws on the experience of the National Domestic Workers Alliance to argue that affection for an individual caregiver is not a substitute for wages, rest, safety, legal protection, or a voice at work. The household can be a site of genuine intimacy and also an employment relationship shaped by large inequalities. Her framing matters for feminism because it connects reproductive labor to labor law, migration policy, racial justice, disability, and aging, rather than treating care as a natural female capacity that families can draw on without public investment.

As a short public talk, it offers a moral and political entry point rather than a detailed account of financing, enforcement, or conflicts within care systems. Its optimism should therefore be paired with research on global care chains and with workers’ own testimony. Still, the video is unusually effective at changing the listener’s field of vision: the crisis is not that society has stopped valuing care in the abstract, but that it depends on caregivers while systematically transferring cost and insecurity onto them. The practical question is how rights, public funding, and collective responsibility can make good care and good jobs possible at the same time.
