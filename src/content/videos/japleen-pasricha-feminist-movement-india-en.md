Japleen Pasricha sketches a history of feminist organizing in India and explains why the movement cannot be imported as a story of Western waves. Colonial rule, anti-colonial nationalism, constitutional reform, autonomous women’s groups, anti-violence campaigns, labor struggles, anti-caste thought, and digital media all shape the field in different ways.

The talk is especially useful as a challenge to the question ‘Is feminism still needed?’ Legal equality does not erase sexual violence, unpaid care, workplace discrimination, caste endogamy, or the policing of queer and trans lives. Pasricha also stresses that the category ‘woman’ is internally unequal. A feminism centered on urban dominant-caste experience can reproduce the exclusions it claims to oppose.

As a short introductory lecture, the chronology necessarily compresses disputes and regional differences. It should lead outward to Dalit, Adivasi, Muslim, queer, disabled, labor, and regional-language feminist archives rather than stand in for them. Its value is orientation: it gives new readers a map while making clear that a movement remains democratic only when those previously treated as its margins can reshape its center.
