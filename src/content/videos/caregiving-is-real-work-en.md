Sharmi Surianarain begins with the roughly 16 billion hours of unpaid care performed around the world each day and challenges workplaces that imagine employees without dependencies. People are simultaneously caring for children, older relatives, illness, and households, and that responsibility is strongly gendered. Employers benefit from the stable workforce care produces while treating care time as a private disruption workers must solve elsewhere.

The talk calls for care to enter the design of work through paid family leave, flexible but predictable arrangements, supportive management, and a culture that does not punish people for using policy. The point is not simply to permit care, but to recognize that capacity changes across a life course. Flexibility available only after disclosing private crisis, proving exceptional need, or sacrificing promotion is not a right; it is an exception dependent on managerial goodwill.

At five minutes, the talk is an organizational intervention rather than a full analysis of low-paid care sectors, informal workers, or public provision. It still supplies a useful test. When employers claim to value wellbeing, examine whether workload, promotion, and leave actually accommodate interdependent lives. A care-friendly workplace cannot remain brand language; it must change who can stay, who can advance, and who pays for the organization’s demands on time.
