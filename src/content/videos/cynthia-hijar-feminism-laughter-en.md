Cynthia Híjar starts from a stereotype: feminists are supposedly humorless because they refuse sexist and racist jokes. She reverses the accusation by showing laughter as a feminist method. Her comic character Nacho Progre makes familiar contradictions visible—the man who speaks equality while expecting women’s labor, attention, or gratitude—and allows audiences to recognize power in ordinary behavior.

Humor works politically because it changes who controls the frame. Sexist comedy turns marginalized people into objects; feminist satire makes entitlement, hypocrisy, and patriarchal common sense available for collective inspection. Shared laughter can reduce isolation and create a language for criticism that travels quickly through social media and daily conversation.

The talk is brief and deliberately accessible, so it does not resolve questions about whose humor circulates, who becomes its target, or when irony reproduces the harm it names. Its contribution is practical: movements need forms that can carry truth and pain without making exhaustion their only emotional register. Joy is not a retreat from politics but part of how people remain capable of changing the world.
