Nina Siren Gualinga speaks from the Ecuadorian Amazon, where forest defense is inseparable from Indigenous collective life. Women appear not as symbolic caretakers of an abstract “nature,” but as political organizers defending water, territory, food systems, culture, and the conditions that allow communities and nonhuman relatives to continue living.

The talk also names the cost of that leadership. Women environmental defenders face surveillance, criminalization, harassment, and gendered violence intended to push them out of public struggle. Extraction reaches through territory into households and bodies; resistance likewise joins legal action, community mobilization, international advocacy, and the transmission of knowledge between generations.

An ecofeminist reading should avoid claiming that women are naturally closer to the earth. Gualinga’s authority comes from political history, community responsibility, and situated knowledge, not biological destiny. The talk is a strong introduction to climate justice because it asks who defines development, who absorbs its violence, and why protecting forests requires respecting Indigenous sovereignty rather than merely managing carbon.
