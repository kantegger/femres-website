Marie Hicks’s *Programmed Inequality* overturns the familiar story that women simply failed to enter computing. In postwar Britain, women already performed much of the state’s essential computational labor. Programming and machine operation were classified as low-status clerical work precisely because women did them, even as government increasingly depended on their expertise. When computing became strategically prestigious, institutions redefined the field, promoted men, and treated experienced women as an expendable labor pool.

The book follows civil-service classifications, hiring policy, training, national computer projects, and the eventual collapse of British technological ambition. Gender discrimination was not only unfair to individuals; it damaged institutional memory and national capacity by excluding the people who knew how systems worked. Hicks therefore shifts technology history from heroic inventors to labor organization: expertise is not discovered neutrally but created or destroyed through pay scales, occupational categories, and managerial beliefs about who looks like a professional.

Because the study centers Britain’s public sector, it does not by itself explain every computing industry or fully resolve how race, empire, class, and colonial labor intersected with gender. Its lesson should also not be reduced to “hire more women” while leaving exploitative hierarchies intact. For FemRes readers, the deeper question is how a technical occupation changes status when its workforce changes, whose work becomes invisible infrastructure, and how institutions program inequality before any line of software is written.
