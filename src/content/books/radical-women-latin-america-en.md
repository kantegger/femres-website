Edited by Victoria González-Rivera and Karen Kampwirth, *Radical Women in Latin America* studies women not only in revolutionary and leftist movements but also in radical right-wing mobilization. Its comparative cases challenge two comforting assumptions: that militant politics is naturally male, and that women’s political participation is naturally progressive. Women organized clandestine networks, armed struggle, neighborhood support, electoral projects, religious activism, and counterrevolution for reasons shaped by class, family, faith, nation, and local histories.

The chapters show how movements rely on gendered labor while limiting women’s authority. Revolutionary organizations may celebrate the female combatant yet restore domestic expectations after victory; conservative movements can mobilize motherhood and moral guardianship to defend hierarchy while giving participants real political skills. Treating women as agents does not require approving their projects. It requires explaining how organizations turn femininity, sacrifice, and family responsibility into political resources and how participants negotiate those scripts.

Published in 2001, the collection reflects the cases and vocabulary available at the time and gives uneven attention to Indigenous, Afro-Latin, queer, and trans politics. “Radical” also covers projects with profoundly different ethical aims, so comparison must not become equivalence. Its value for FemRes is to separate political agency from automatic emancipation and to ask a harder question of every movement: what gender order does it build while pursuing revolution, democracy, religion, or national restoration?
