*Fresh Banana Leaves* begins with a structural contradiction: Indigenous peoples suffer some of the gravest climate harms and steward extraordinary biodiversity, yet mainstream environmental policy still excludes Indigenous science from the category of authoritative knowledge. Maya Ch’orti’ and Binnizá environmental scientist Jessica Hernandez brings family memory, scientific training, community cases, and colonial history together, refusing to reduce Indigenous knowledge to cultural material that outside researchers may extract.

Indigenous science here is not a static store of ancient custom. It is knowledge built through sustained observation, practical testing, intergenerational transmission, and obligations among relatives. Land, water, plants, animals, and humans are not separable variables, and stewardship cannot be reduced to yield or “ecosystem services.” This relational approach unsettles the lines conventionally drawn between laboratory and community, expert and resident, objective data and spiritual ethics.

“Ecocolonialism” is the book’s sharpest concept. Western conservation often narrates national parks as empty wilderness while concealing the removal of Indigenous people and the prohibition of burning, gathering, and rotational land practices. Even institutions that now acknowledge traditional ecological knowledge may extract useful data without returning land, authority, or resources. Decolonization cannot therefore mean inviting a few advisers into a project whose goals have already been fixed.

Cases of urban Indigenous restoration in Seattle, environmental violence in Latin America, and Hernandez’s family history ground the argument in migration, war, and food systems. Banana plants and leaves register the exploitation of colonial plantations, but also the ways displaced peoples build kinship with new land. Ancestral foods are not nostalgic decoration; they are infrastructure connecting seed, labor, language, health, and territorial sovereignty.

Hernandez centers Indigenous women and land defenders without settling for an ecofeminist romance in which women are naturally closer to nature. Women’s responsibilities for food, care, and cultural transmission, and their exposure to extractive violence, are produced through colonial gender orders rather than biology. Environmental justice therefore has to be read together with gender, race, class, and sovereignty.

The book’s forceful contrast between “Western” and “Indigenous” sciences can at moments flatten diversity within both, and no single volume can substitute for each nation’s own protocols, gender relations, and political disagreements. Its weave of memoir and large historical claims makes it a political and epistemological intervention rather than a conservation manual. Readers should resist turning its lessons into another universal toolkit detached from the communities that authorize knowledge.

For FemRes, *Fresh Banana Leaves* shifts climate politics from carbon metrics back to land return, epistemic authority, and repaired relationships. It asks environmental organizations to begin with different questions: who defines the crisis, who owns the land, who can refuse a project, and who pays for a “green transition”? Indigenous leadership is not a diversity supplement; it requires transferring resources, time, and decision-making power to communities already caring for the land.
