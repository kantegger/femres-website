Lila Abu-Lughod takes aim at a question that became politically powerful after 11 September 2001: can military intervention be justified as the liberation of Muslim women? Her answer begins with anthropology’s demand for specificity. “Muslim women” do not form a single social type, and a garment, custom, or religious practice cannot explain lives shaped by war, poverty, authoritarian government, occupation, kinship, aspiration, and global consumer culture.

The book examines how rescue stories organize moral distance. The rescuer appears modern, free, and benevolent; the woman to be saved appears voiceless and trapped by her culture. This structure allows Western audiences to feel concern without confronting the foreign policies, arms economies, border regimes, and economic inequalities in which their own states participate. Compassion becomes politically selective when it focuses on cultural symbols but ignores the conditions that make life precarious.

Abu-Lughod does not romanticize local communities or deny gendered violence. She insists that critique must begin from women’s actual circumstances, including the compromises and attachments through which they make a life. Freedom is not a single measure of individual choice exported from elsewhere. Justice may require security, housing, education, labor rights, political sovereignty, or an end to bombing before it resembles the liberation imagined by an international campaign.

The argument has generated productive debate. A critique of rescue can itself become too cautious if it discourages transnational solidarity or leaves local patriarchies unchallenged. The book’s stronger lesson is not nonintervention in every sense; it is accountability about who defines harm, whose knowledge guides action, and what material consequences follow.

For FemRes, the book is a method for reading humanitarian images and policy language. Whenever a woman’s suffering is presented as proof that an entire culture is backward, readers should ask what history has been removed from the frame and whose power is enlarged by the proposed solution. Solidarity begins not with saving an abstract other but with listening, redistribution, and political responsibility.
