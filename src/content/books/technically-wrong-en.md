*Technically Wrong* examines the small interfaces through which technological power becomes ordinary: registration forms that cannot represent queer families, health tools built around narrow assumptions about bodies, platforms that surface painful memories, chatbots trained into harassment, and risk systems that reproduce racial punishment. Sara Wachter-Boettcher’s point is not that each failure is an isolated bug. Product teams repeatedly imagine a frictionless “normal user,” encode that person into defaults and data fields, and then describe the exclusion of everyone else as an edge case. Design therefore becomes a quiet system for deciding whose life is anticipated and whose must continually request an exception.

The book connects harmful products to the labor culture that produces them. Homogeneous teams, heroic-founder myths, growth metrics, and the habit of moving fast all make it easier to treat ethical reflection as an obstacle rather than part of technical competence. From a feminist perspective, this shifts the question beyond adding more women to an unchanged industry. Representation matters, but accountability also requires changing research practices, decision-making power, testing, content strategy, and the business incentives that reward extraction. A humane interface is not merely polite copy; it is evidence that designers have considered grief, disability, racialization, gender variance, and the possibility that users may be vulnerable to the system itself.

Published before generative AI became a mass product, some examples now look like an earlier layer of digital life. That distance makes the method more, not less, useful: contemporary AI systems still inherit categories, datasets, organizational incentives, and imagined users. The book is strongest as an accessible bridge between everyday frustration and structural critique, though it is less comprehensive on platform labor, surveillance, and global supply chains. For FemRes readers, it offers a practical habit of attention—ask who defined the default, who benefits from speed, who absorbs the error, and whether the people harmed have real authority to change the product.
