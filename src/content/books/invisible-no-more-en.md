*Invisible No More* addresses a double absence in accounts of police violence. Mainstream antiracist narratives often presume a Black male victim, while mainstream gender-violence advocacy often imagines the state as women’s protector. Andrea J. Ritchie centers Black women, Indigenous women, other women of color, and trans and gender-nonconforming people to show that they are not marginal exceptions to state violence. Their cases seem rare not because violence is rare, but because reporting systems, news coverage, statistics, litigation, and movement memory jointly determine who can become a recognizable “victim of police violence.”

Ritchie begins with institutional genealogies reaching back to slavery and colonization. Slave patrols, Indigenous dispossession, immigration enforcement, and the policing of poor people did not merely regulate labor or territory; they disciplined gender, sexuality, kinship, and reproduction. Black women could be imagined as too strong to be injured and simultaneously too unruly not to be controlled. Indigenous women’s bodies were placed in jurisdictional gaps created by colonial rule, while anyone outside white feminine respectability was more readily interpreted as a threat. This history turns contemporary incidents from the aberrations of a few “bad cops” into continuations of an old governing paradigm.

The book places the lives of Sandra Bland, Rekia Boyd, Dajerria Becton, Monica Jones, Mya Hall, Eleanor Bumpurs, Kayla Moore, and many others within that structure. Their encounters include traffic stops, fatal shootings, school and street discipline, presumptions of prostitution, responses to disability crises, and violence against trans bodies. Naming them is not the creation of a tragic roll call. The cases reveal recurring police judgments: whose anger is read as aggression, whose request for help becomes resistance, whose mere presence is treated as suspicious, and whose death quickly disappears because it does not resemble the publicly familiar victim.

Ritchie organizes the evidence through policing girls, disability, police sexual violence, gender lines, sex work, motherhood, and police responses to violence. A defining structure emerges: women of color are intensively monitored in streets, schools, welfare, and family systems, yet underprotected when they experience domestic violence, sexual assault, or police abuse. A Black mother may be punished for allegedly failing to protect a child and treated as defiant when she actively protects one. Sex workers are subject to frequent stops while their reports of assault remain disbelieved. “Hypervisible/invisible” is not a contradiction; power chooses when to see a body and when to erase its injury.

As a police-misconduct lawyer, researcher, and organizer, Ritchie joins legal records, journalism, interviews, movement archives, and personal experience. The method does more than add women’s cases to an existing field: it changes the definition of police violence. Sexual humiliation, invasive searches, threats to custody, coercive responses to disability, and immigration enforcement must be analyzed alongside shootings and deaths in custody. She also documents how movements such as #SayHerName contest the politics of memory, turning erased names into starting points for organizing, policy demands, and coalitions across difference.

The book’s sharpest feminist challenge is that an anti-violence movement cannot oppose male violence while transferring ever more power to police who enact gendered violence themselves. Training, women officers, body cameras, or “community policing” may alter procedures without ending the criminalization of Black girls, sex workers, trans people, disabled people, and migrants. Ritchie does not ask readers to ignore immediate danger. She demands that the resource architecture of safety become visible: are housing, health care, income, immigration security, peer crisis response, and survivor-led support funded with anything like the commitment devoted to enforcement?

The book ranges across centuries, populations, and modes of policing, and that breadth entails trade-offs. Many cases receive limited space; differences among Indigenous nations, migrant communities, and regional policing histories cannot be fully developed. Work after 2017 on Black trans life, pandemic enforcement, the 2020 uprising, and police budgets must extend the account, while community language and organizing strategies continue to change. Readers should not flatten the people here into a homogeneous “women-of-color experience,” but use Ritchie’s intersectional method to identify how difference changes both the violence and the support required.

Two lines offer a useful reading route: trace institutional continuity from colonial legacies to modern “criminalizing webs,” then compare how disability, sexuality, motherhood, and gender expression alter police encounters. Pair the book with *Arrested Justice* for the matrix between interpersonal male violence and state violence, then with *Pushout* and *No More Police* to connect school discipline and alternative safety. For FemRes, it offers more than names that deserve inclusion. It offers an editorial rule: whenever a public category claims to represent “women” or “Black people,” identify its presumed subject and ask whether those at the margins are missing because of a data gap—or because an institution has already accomplished their erasure.
