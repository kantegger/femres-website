*Golden Gulag* begins from an apparent contradiction: from the 1980s onward, California undertook what state analysts called the largest prison-building project in history while crime rates were falling over the long term. Ruth Wilson Gilmore refuses explanations that rest on a sudden rise in public fear, uniquely malicious politicians, or racial prejudice treated only as an endpoint. She asks how a wealthy state with a progressive self-image mobilized land, bonds, labor, and administrative capacity over several decades until cages became an ordinary instrument for governing economic and social crisis.

Her answer is organized around the celebrated analysis of four surpluses: finance capital, land, labor, and state capacity. Changes in Cold War spending, agricultural restructuring, industrial decline, and fiscal crisis left capital seeking relatively secure investments, land seeking new uses, displaced workers and unemployed populations politically reclassified, and state agencies searching for missions through which to reproduce themselves. Prison construction temporarily joined these elements in a “prison fix”: public debt financed building, rural communities were promised jobs, and the injuries of urban poverty and unemployment were redescribed as problems of crime and dangerousness.

Gilmore’s method matters because it genuinely joins political economy to geography. The book moves among state budgets, Central Valley land and water, Los Angeles neighborhoods, and families linked across distance by incarceration. A prison is never an isolated building. It depends on legislation, bonds, county lobbying, labor interests, police, and transportation networks, and it reshapes both the town receiving the facility and the urban community from which people are removed. The place where “crime happens” and the place where punishment is built may be hundreds of miles apart, yet finance, visiting labor, and family separation bind them into one map.

This account also corrects the promise that prisons rescue declining rural economies. Construction does not necessarily create enough stable jobs for local residents and may instead consume land, water, infrastructure, and long-term public commitments. Incarcerated people are counted statistically where the prison stands while denied political participation there, distorting both representation and resources. Mass incarceration does not eliminate social costs; it redistributes them between city and countryside, Black communities and predominantly white districts, public services and punishment budgets.

The book’s feminist force becomes especially clear in its history of Mothers Reclaiming Our Children, or Mothers ROC. Confronting “three strikes” laws and other punitive measures, Los Angeles mothers transformed what the state treated as private grief into public politics: accompanying people to court, learning law, connecting families, organizing across neighborhoods, and refusing the classification of imprisoned children as disposable. Gilmore does not essentialize motherhood as an innate moral virtue. She shows how care can be collectively organized, extended beyond the household, and turned into a practice that contests the state’s production of abandoned populations.

Abolition in *Golden Gulag* is therefore not the moral gesture of closing a handful of prisons but a reorganization of the resources through which life and safety are made. If prison expansion absorbs finance, land, labor, and state capacity, reverse engineering means redirecting those capacities toward schools, housing, health care, transit, income, and community organization. Gilmore’s later formulation that abolition is about presence, not merely absence, is already demonstrated empirically here: reducing imprisonment will not become another abandonment only when institutions exist that no longer process crisis through cages.

It is also a demanding book. Its close treatment of bond finance, Californian agriculture, administrative restructuring, and regional politics makes the argument far more complex than the shorthand “crime fell while prisons grew.” Readers unfamiliar with US state government may lose the line among the evidence. The focus on California in the 1980s and 1990s cannot by itself explain later expansions in immigration detention, electronic monitoring, predictive policing, or private digital surveillance. Gender is not an explicit axis in every chapter, so feminist readers must continue the analysis through family separation, reproductive labor, and the organizing of Mothers ROC.

The best route is not to skip the political economy for the conclusion, but to trace how crisis produces surplus, how prisons connect those surpluses into a policy solution, and how opposition reorganizes relationships within the same geography. Pair it with *The New Jim Crow*: Michelle Alexander foregrounds law and racial caste, while Gilmore explains the material construction of prison expansion. Then read *No More Police* and *Beyond Survival* to see how resource redistribution becomes a safety practice. For FemRes, the enduring lesson is that every punitive institution described as inevitable should prompt two questions: who organized which available resources to create it, and what forms of life those resources might have sustained instead.
