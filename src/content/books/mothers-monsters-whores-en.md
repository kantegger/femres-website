Laura Sjoberg and Caron E. Gentry begin *Mothers, Monsters, Whores* from a recurring refusal in global politics: when women commit political violence, public explanation often denies that they acted as intelligible political agents. The “mother” is driven by family or maternal instinct, the “monster” is unnaturally violent, and the “whore” is manipulated through deviant sexuality. Each story restores violence to masculinity by making the violent woman an exception to proper womanhood.

Through cases involving terrorists, combatants, torturers, and perpetrators of genocide, the authors compare media, policy, and scholarly narratives with women’s stated motives and political contexts. Their argument is not that violent women are emancipated, nor that agency removes coercion. It is that explanation becomes inaccurate when gender stereotypes replace analysis of ideology, organization, strategy, trauma, and opportunity. Feminism must be able to recognize women as capable of harm without reproducing misogynist dehumanization.

The three-part typology is intentionally sharp and can itself risk fixing diverse lives into memorable labels; later work also demands greater attention to race, colonial power, sexuality, and the unequal credibility granted to different women. The book remains useful because it identifies a trap in representation. Treating women only as peaceful victims may appear sympathetic, but it makes both their violence and their resistance politically unintelligible—and leaves the gendered institutions that organize violence unexamined.
