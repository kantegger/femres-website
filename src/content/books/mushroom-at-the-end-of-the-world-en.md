Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing follows the matsutake mushroom from disturbed forests through pickers, buyers, exporters, and Japanese gift markets to ask how life continues in landscapes damaged by capitalism. Matsutake resists industrial cultivation and flourishes through contingent relations among pine, fungi, people, fire, and ruined land. The mushroom becomes a guide to “precarity”: not an exceptional crisis affecting unfortunate individuals, but the shared condition of living without guarantees in worlds no actor fully controls.

The supply chain links Oregon pickers—including refugees and displaced workers—to highly valued commodities without becoming a stable factory system. Tsing calls attention to “salvage accumulation,” through which capital captures value produced in social and ecological relations it did not organize. Her polyphonic method also contests progress narratives: rather than one universal timeline of modernization, the book follows multiple histories, rhythms, and forms of collaboration that emerge inside capitalist ruins.

The lyrical focus on indeterminacy can risk aestheticizing precarity or leaving coercive differences in property, race, citizenship, and labor less systematized than some readers need. More-than-human collaboration should not be mistaken for social harmony, nor survival for justice. The book’s feminist political-ecological value lies in changing the scale of attention: it teaches readers to notice maintenance, interdependence, and histories erased by the fantasy of autonomous production, while still asking who is forced to improvise and who profits from that improvisation.
