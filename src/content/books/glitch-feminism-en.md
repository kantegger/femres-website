Legacy Russell’s *Glitch Feminism* begins with a counterintuitive proposition: a glitch need not be an error awaiting repair; it can be the moment a body refuses a system’s classification. Writing after the boundary between online and everyday life has collapsed, Russell rejects the idea that digital identity is false while the offline body is real. For Black, queer, trans, and gender-nonconforming people, screens, avatars, handles, and virtual spaces have offered means to rehearse selfhood, alter visibility, and find community. A digital body is therefore a body, not a cheap substitute for reality.

The glitch operates as both technical metaphor and political method. Normative systems demand bodies that are legible, stable, and archivable: forms require one gender, platforms convert desire into data, and states and markets distribute rights, risk, and advertising through categories. Refusing to render correctly, moving among identities, or frustrating recognition exposes “normal” as something designed rather than natural. Russell’s Black queer perspective is crucial here: institutions often identify as errors not their own limits but bodies that fail white, heterosexual, and cisgender standards.

Contemporary artists and online makers carry much of the book’s thought. Distorted images, avatars, performance, collage, and digital ghosts do not merely illustrate theory; they test whether bodies can exceed their assigned borders. Russell’s manifesto-like prose connects refusal, ghosting, encryption, and other gestures of interruption. Creation becomes more than a demand for accurate representation inside an existing system. It asks who owns the power to name, display, and watch in the first place.

Yet a glitch is not automatically liberatory. Platforms turn subculture into content and identity experiments into profiles; surveillance, harassment, moderation, and algorithmic bias impose unequal costs. Digital life also rests on minerals, warehouses, content moderation, low-paid delivery, and unequal access—material conditions the book does not explore at equal depth. A glitch rapidly patched or commodified may even teach a platform how to absorb dissent more efficiently.

*Glitch Feminism* is therefore best read as an aesthetic-political manifesto, not a regulatory manual or comprehensive history of digital labor. Its brief chapters, aphorisms, and conceptual leaps generate energy but may frustrate readers seeking institutional programs. For FemRes, the task is not to romanticize every error but to extend Russell’s questions: who defines correct output, who is classified as noise, who controls the infrastructure of repair, and can we preserve a self that need not become fully legible to a platform?
