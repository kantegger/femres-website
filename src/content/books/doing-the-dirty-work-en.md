Based on original research and Bridget Anderson’s involvement in campaigns with migrant domestic workers, *Doing the Dirty Work?* asks what is actually sold when paid work enters a private home. Its field spans Southern and Northern Europe, the United Kingdom, and the legacy of domestic service in the United States. The answer cannot be reduced to a list of cleaning and caring tasks, because employers often purchase control over time, movement, demeanor, and personhood.

Anderson begins by challenging a feminist fiction: that housework is a common burden imposed on all women in roughly the same way. Patriarchy makes domestic labor women’s responsibility, but race, class, nationality, and citizenship determine which women can transfer that responsibility to others and on what terms. The liberation of a professional woman can coexist with the subordination of a migrant worker inside her home.

Domestic work is difficult to define precisely because it combines physical maintenance, bodily care, emotional management, and the reproduction of social status. “Dirt” is not merely matter to be removed; societies assign some people to handle what others mark as contaminating, intimate, degrading, or close to death. The worker is consequently asked to perform a subordinate identity as well as a service, making the sale of labor resemble a sale of the self.

Comparing employment patterns across Europe, Anderson shows how border and labor regimes manufacture vulnerability. A worker whose visa, housing, or right to remain depends on an employer cannot bargain like an abstractly free contractor. The chapter title “Your Passport Is Your Life” captures how the state enters the household: immigration control gives private employers leverage that an employment contract alone cannot explain.

The promise that a worker is “just like one of the family” is similarly double-edged. Affection and intimacy can be real, but family language also makes fixed hours, job descriptions, privacy, overtime, and dismissal procedures seem cold or inappropriate. The worker is invited to give loyalty beyond contract while remaining outside the inheritance, security, and equal standing that family membership would ordinarily imply.

By connecting contemporary service to the legacy of slavery and racialized domestic labor in the American South, Anderson shows that this hierarchy is not newly created by globalization. Old images of the naturally servile or caring woman are adapted to new nationalities and migration routes. The analysis therefore unsettles any automatic sisterhood between employer and worker: shared gender does not dissolve power over wages, residence, or deportability.

Published in 2000, the book’s empirical map predates major changes in European migration law, domestic-worker organizing, platform hiring, and digital surveillance. Its language often follows a women/North/South framework that later queer, trans, and more regionally specific scholarship can complicate. The cases should not be used to claim that every household relation is identical; their force lies in revealing recurrent mechanisms that current evidence must update.

Read *Doing the Dirty Work?* along two parallel lines. First list the task, skill, hours, and wage named in a contract; then list the demanded status—obedience, gratitude, availability, intimacy, silence—and ask which law makes that demand enforceable. Pair the result with current worker-led research in the country concerned. The book’s enduring lesson is that decent domestic work requires labor rights and migration rights together; reforming only one leaves the household hierarchy intact.
