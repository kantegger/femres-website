*Gendered Trajectories* opens with a comparative puzzle. Japan has been wealthier and developed more extensive welfare provision, yet improvement in women’s economic position has long moved more slowly than in Taiwan. The two societies have intertwined histories, related family norms, and patriarchal cultures, so their divergence cannot be explained simply by degrees of tradition. Wei-hsin Yu turns instead to firms, labor markets, and industrial policy, asking how institutions widen or narrow inequality at critical points in women’s lives.

The book refuses to measure progress through a single year’s female employment rate. A work trajectory consists of entry, marriage, childbirth, exit, return, and promotion, and every decision reshapes what will be available later. In Japan, long-term employment, internal promotion, and intense corporate commitment were designed around a male career without care interruption. Women entering large firms were more likely to be placed on tracks with less training and promotion; after marriage or childbirth, return often meant irregular and low-paid employment.

Taiwan’s trajectory shows how weaker internal labor markets, a larger small-firm sector, and different industrial policies created more routes for women to remain or return. This does not mean Taiwan achieved equality, nor that flexibility is inherently liberating. More openings can coexist with weaker security and new labor risks. The important finding is that organizational forms translate similar gender expectations into different career consequences.

Combining historical trends, survey evidence, and interviews, Yu returns individual choice to institutional context. A woman who “chooses” to leave after childbirth may face unpredictable hours, missing childcare, a husband’s higher wage, and a firm rewarding uninterrupted tenure. Years later, the resulting gap is treated as evidence of lower ability. Early differences accumulate into lifetime inequalities in earnings, position, pensions, and bargaining power at home. Gender inequality is not one discriminatory event but a path-dependent trajectory.

The comparison also refuses Western experience as the universal sequence of industrial society. Growth, welfare expansion, and modernization do not automatically produce one path to women’s emancipation. Institutions may protect “family” by reinforcing the male-breadwinner model, or celebrate market flexibility while transferring risk to women. Effective reform must join childcare, working time, promotion, social insurance, and irregular employment rather than asking women alone to become more competitive.

The evidence centers on the latter twentieth century and early twenty-first, so it cannot directly answer platform work, newer low-fertility policy, pandemic disruption, or queer and trans experience. Differences of class, ethnicity, and migration within both societies also deserve more development. The method remains powerful: comparison is not a ranking of national progress but a way to identify institutions through which gender gaps accumulate over time. The book supplies a strong historical foundation for understanding motherhood penalties, occupational tracking, and the politics of care in East Asian work.
