Vandana Shiva’s *Making Peace with the Earth* organizes ecological crisis as a series of connected wars over land, water, climate, forests, food, and seed. Corporate globalization presents extraction, genetic engineering, industrial agriculture, and land acquisition as development, while transferring ecological debt to farmers, Indigenous communities, women, and future generations. Shiva’s language of war is meant to reveal that environmental destruction is not an accidental side effect of growth: laws, trade regimes, and property rights actively authorize some institutions to exhaust the conditions of collective life.

The book’s ecofeminist force lies in connecting biodiversity and subsistence to political economy. Seed sovereignty concerns who may reproduce life and knowledge; water and forests are relations of survival before they are commodities. Women often appear at the center not because they are naturally closer to nature, but because gendered divisions of food production, fuel, water, and care make them encounter dispossession early and organize against it. Earth-centered democracy therefore requires collective control, ecological limits, and the authority of local knowledge rather than greener consumer branding.

Shiva’s broad civilizational contrasts can nevertheless compress differences among communities and sometimes risk opposing a pure local world to a uniformly destructive West. Readers should supplement the book with scholarship attentive to caste, labor, state power, scientific disagreement, and Indigenous sovereignty on its own terms. Its strongest contribution is not a romantic return to tradition but a demanding political test: who bears the damage of “growth,” whose knowledge is dismissed, and can an economy count the reproduction of soil, water, seed, and care before corporate profit?
