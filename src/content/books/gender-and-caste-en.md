*Gender and Caste* is not a conventional collection that merely adds “women” to caste studies. Anupama Rao assembled these texts to unsettle two persistent assumptions: that feminism can treat women as a shared subject untouched by caste, and that anti-caste politics can postpone gender until some later stage of liberation. First issued in India in 2003 as the opening volume in Kali for Women’s *Issues in Contemporary Indian Feminism* series, and published internationally by Zed Books in 2005, the anthology makes caste a constitutive problem for feminist thought rather than one specialized topic within it.

More than twenty essays, manifestos, interviews, reports, and testimonies form a deliberately unsettled intellectual history. Meenakshi Moon and Urmila Pawar recover women in the early untouchable liberation movement; Gopal Guru asks why “Dalit women talk differently”; Sharmila Rege develops a Dalit feminist standpoint; and Uma Chakravarti traces Phule, Brahmanism, and Brahmanical patriarchy. Later sections on land, agricultural labor, law, atrocity, ethnography, and the Hindu Code Bill return those conceptual arguments to material institutions. The collection’s breadth is not ornamental: it shows that caste-gender power changes form as it moves between household, movement, village, court, and archive.

Brahmanical patriarchy, in this account, is more than a slogan placing caste beside gender. Caste reproduction depends upon endogamy, lineage, inheritance, and ideas of purity; regulating women’s sexuality, marriage, and reproductive capacity is therefore infrastructure for maintaining hierarchy. Savarna women may be subordinated within patriarchal families while also receiving status from caste boundaries they help reproduce. Dalit women face dominant-caste sexual violence, stigmatized labor, and dispossession, as well as male dominance within their own communities. Once those relations are visible, the claim that all women experience one common patriarchy becomes analytically untenable.

The anthology’s sharper intervention is to make Dalit feminism question two movements at once. Mainstream Indian feminism, especially where shaped by dominant-caste women, has often universalized its own experience of family and rights. Anti-caste movements have sometimes treated criticism of internal gender violence as a threat to collective unity. A Dalit feminist standpoint does not claim that identity automatically produces truth. It argues that people situated where caste, gender, and class power meet can perceive relations systematically hidden by single-axis frameworks. Solidarity cannot be built by requiring one part of a movement to make its injuries politically inconvenient.

Throughout the volume, bodily harm remains tied to material resources. Chapters on the Sirasgaon atrocity, Dalit agricultural workers, land, and law show how sexual violence can enforce caste authority, discipline labor, and punish collective challenge rather than simply express private misogyny. Formal equality is then filtered through police conduct, village power, evidentiary rules, and access to property. Whether a woman owns land, can refuse degrading work, or is believed when she testifies may reveal more about the substance of rights than a state’s promise to “protect women.”

Rao’s editorial method is itself a feminist argument. Scholars do not enjoy an uncontested right to interpret Dalit women from above: movement documents, oral histories, first-person narratives, journalism, and academic analysis occupy the same field of thought. The arrangement forces readers to ask why one woman’s account is classified as raw experience while another person’s conceptual vocabulary becomes universal theory. Knowledge production can reproduce caste when testimony supplies data but its speakers are denied intellectual authority. The anthology preserves disagreement rather than smoothing it away, keeping representation, translation, and speaking position visible.

The book also bears the limits of its moment and archive. Most contributions emerged from late-twentieth-century Indian debates; religious minorities, Adivasi communities, queer and trans lives, and regional differences do not receive equal attention. Its mixed genres and dates sometimes leave readers to construct transitions themselves. Savarna readers should not turn exposure to Dalit testimony into a certificate of moral completion, while international readers should resist treating caste as simply another name for race. Comparison can reveal shared technologies of domination, but equivalence can erase the histories and political vocabularies that make each system distinct.

A productive route is to begin with Guru, Rege, and Chakravarti to establish the arguments about difference, standpoint, and Brahmanical patriarchy; then read the movement histories and testimonies by Moon, Pawar, and the eight Dalit women to test what those concepts illuminate; finally move to law, land, and atrocity to see hierarchy become institutional practice. Paired with Bama’s *Sangati* or Baby Kamble’s *The Prisons We Broke*, the collection becomes especially powerful: those works make collective life audible, while Rao’s anthology explains why some lives were so long denied recognition as sources of feminist knowledge.
