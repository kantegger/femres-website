Uma Chakravarti’s *Gendering Caste* dismantles a convenient but dangerous assumption: that caste is an inherited background hierarchy while gender is a separate inequality. She reconstructs caste as a historical order continually produced through consent, exclusion, punishment, and force. A feminist lens therefore does not merely add women to caste history. It reveals that caste boundaries could not reproduce themselves without governing marriage, sexuality, kinship, inheritance, and childbirth.

The book’s most influential formulation is “Brahmanical patriarchy.” The status and protection available to dominant-caste women are conditional on obedience, chastity, endogamy, and controlled inheritance; policing their sexuality secures property and lineage through approved male lines. Chastity is thus not private morality but political technology. Family, religious authority, and community sanction turn women’s bodies into borders of caste purity, with so-called honor violence representing an extreme rather than an aberrant expression of the system.

Chakravarti also refuses the idea that all women inhabit one undifferentiated patriarchy. Caste location helps determine who is secluded, who must labor, whose body is made sexually available, and who can receive limited privilege through claims to purity. Dominant-caste women face intense sexual regulation, while Dalit and subordinated-caste women frequently confront both labor extraction and sexual violence. This differentiated account makes feminism face an uncomfortable relation: the relative security of some women can rest on the labor and stigmatization of others.

The historical arc moves from early Brahmanical texts and the formation of patriarchal institutions through precolonial, colonial, and contemporary India. Colonial codification, social reform, and nationalism did not simply abolish an old order; they remade tradition, family, and the respectable woman. Formal equality likewise cannot by itself undo endogamy, property arrangements, or community sanctions because caste is reproduced in everyday kinship. Anti-caste thought and feminist critique must therefore enter the same history rather than treating emancipation as a single line of modernization.

This is a deliberately compressed synthesis, unable to represent every regional and community history, and its binary gender vocabulary needs supplementation from Dalit-Bahujan, Adivasi, queer, and trans scholarship. “Brahmanical patriarchy” should not become a static label that explains everything in advance. Its real value is methodological: whenever a marriage rule, moral panic, or family institution appears, ask which caste boundary it protects, whose labor it allocates, and who pays the price for crossing it.
