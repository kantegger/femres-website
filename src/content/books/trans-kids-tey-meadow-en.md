Tey Meadow’s *Trans Kids* is an ethnographic and interview-based study of an early generation of families who affirmed children’s gender nonconformity rather than seeking psychiatric “cure.” Parents adopt new names and pronouns, negotiate clothing and school life, enter gender clinics and support groups, and sometimes petition the state to alter passports or birth certificates. Meadow follows these families not to decide whether a child’s identity is authentic, but to study the social processes through which gender becomes intelligible, credible, and institutionally actionable.

The book complicates the claim that affirmation simply frees a natural identity from social control. Clinics, parental narratives, diagnostic language, law, and advocacy all help produce the recognizable category of the trans child. This does not make children unreal; it shows that every gender, including normative gender, is stabilized through institutions and stories. Parents can protect children while also becoming translators and gatekeepers whose accounts carry more authority than children’s own. Meadow’s attention to anxiety, expertise, and the parent movement gives feminist readers a way beyond the false choice between family autonomy and state control: both can enable agency and impose norms.

Because the study captures a particular historical moment and families able or willing to access affirming networks, it cannot represent children facing poverty, racialized surveillance, disability, family rejection, or different cultural frameworks. Rapid changes in medicine and law also mean it should be read as sociology, not current clinical guidance. Its enduring contribution is analytical. *Trans Kids* shows how institutions decide which childhoods can be narrated as coherent and deserving of care. For educators, families, and advocates, it encourages support that listens to children while remaining critical of the scripts adults require them to perform.
