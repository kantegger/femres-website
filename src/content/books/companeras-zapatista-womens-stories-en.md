Compañeras gathers the accounts of Indigenous women who entered, sustained, and transformed the Zapatista movement in Chiapas. Hilary Klein traces a political history through grandmothers, mothers, and daughters who became insurgents, community authorities, teachers, healers, and negotiators. The result shifts attention away from iconic male spokespeople and toward the everyday collective labor through which autonomy is actually built.

The Revolutionary Women’s Law is an important reference point, but the book does not present a declaration as completed liberation. Women describe challenging forced marriage, exclusion from assemblies, unequal access to education and health care, domestic confinement, and the assumption that cooking and childcare are naturally theirs. Political change occurs through community debate, practical institutions, and repeated confrontation with habits that survive even inside an anticapitalist rebellion.

One of the book’s strengths is its attention to the relationship between gender and collective self-determination. Zapatista women do not frame freedom as individual escape from Indigenous communities. They struggle for land, language, communal government, and resistance to the Mexican state while also contesting patriarchal authority within their own movement. This refuses the colonial choice between defending culture and defending women.

Klein lived and worked in Zapatista communities, and the project grew from testimony collections requested by women in the movement. Nevertheless, any English-language volume edited for an outside audience involves mediation: selection, translation, and the expectations of international solidarity. Readers should remain alert to romanticizing either Indigenous community or armed rebellion, and should treat the narrators as political thinkers rather than symbols of authenticity.

For organizers, the book offers a demanding account of transformation without purity. Movements inherit the social relations they oppose; declarations matter only when they alter authority, time, safety, and material resources. Compañeras is most useful when read as a record of women building autonomy through institutions and relationships, not as a finished story about a revolution that solved gender.
