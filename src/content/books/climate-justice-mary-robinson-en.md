*Climate Justice* begins not with a climate model but with a distributive question. Those most responsible for the crisis generally possess the strongest means of protection, while those who emitted least are the first to lose crops, homes, water, land, and choices about movement. A former president of Ireland and UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Mary Robinson reframes climate change as a human-rights issue. The danger lies not only in rising temperatures, but in the way poverty, colonial history, gendered labor, and political representation determine exposure, compensation, and participation in solutions.

The book proceeds through encounters and named lives. Ugandan farmer Constance Okollet watches irregular weather destroy livelihoods, then helps her community plant trees, store water, and adapt agricultural practice. In East Biloxi, Mississippi, Sharon Hanshaw organizes residents after Hurricane Katrina from her beauty salon and eventually carries local experience to the United Nations. Robinson also visits Alaska, Chad, Vietnam, Sámi reindeer lands, and Kiribati, where sea-level rise threatens national territory. The abstract “vulnerable population” becomes a set of people with strategies and political judgment.

Women recur in these accounts not because they are naturally closer to nature, but because social divisions of labor assign them disproportionate responsibility for water, food cultivation, children, elders, and household health. Longer journeys for water, damaged food systems, and male out-migration rapidly enlarge unpaid work; unequal access to land, credit, technology, and formal authority then constrains adaptation. A feminist reading should therefore resist celebrating women as innately “resilient” and examine how risk is transferred through reproductive labor.

Robinson also insists that frontline communities are not victims waiting for northern experts. They already develop seed practices, energy systems, building methods, mutual aid, and policy demands, and they claim places at negotiating tables. A “seat at the table” is not merely a higher count of women representatives. It means treating lived experience as knowledge and subjecting climate finance and technological choices to the judgment of affected communities. The path from local initiative to global institutions offers a feminist warning: participation without resources and decision-making power becomes consultation theater.

Migration and “just transition” widen the scale of justice. Sea-level rise may force whole island populations to relocate, but “migration with dignity” differs from managing people as refugees; it concerns culture, sovereignty, family ties, and choice. Likewise, ending fossil-fuel dependence cannot simply abandon workers whose livelihoods depend on those industries. Climate policy must address historical emissions, financial responsibility, labor guarantees, food, and energy access, or a green transition will reproduce older class and colonial relations.

The book’s strength is also its limit. Robinson’s accessible stories and ethic of hope open a complicated subject, but give less sustained analysis to fossil capital, corporate power, imperialism, and global finance as systems producing the crisis. A globally prominent former head of state retelling grassroots women’s stories also raises the question of who speaks for whom. “Resilience,” if appropriated by policymakers, can become a virtue demanded of those left to adapt alone. The narratives should open structural inquiry, not let inspiration substitute for power analysis.

The enduring proposition of *Climate Justice* is that every climate policy must answer three questions: who owes responsibility, who receives resources, and who has a voice. It is a strong public entry point into climate feminism when read alongside work on Indigenous sovereignty, environmental racism, care economies, and political ecology. Hope then means more than personal optimism; it names the political possibility that frontline knowledge might change budgets, laws, infrastructure, and the international distribution of obligation.
