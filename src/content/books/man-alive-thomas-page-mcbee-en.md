Thomas Page McBee’s *Man Alive* is a memoir of becoming a man shaped by two episodes of male violence: childhood sexual abuse by his father and an attempted street murder by a stranger. Transition does not supply a neat before-and-after resolution. Instead, McBee asks how he can desire recognition as male without inheriting the silence, domination, and emotional foreclosure that have harmed him. The book makes masculinity an ethical problem rather than treating transness as a medical puzzle.

Its braided structure moves among memory, investigation, forgiveness, bodily change, and the search for the man who held him at gunpoint. McBee distinguishes understanding violence from excusing it and refuses the idea that survival requires either permanent victimhood or heroic conquest. Encounters with other men reveal masculinity as learned conduct—gestures, permissions, fears, and forms of care—so transition becomes an unusually conscious opportunity to decide what kind of man to practice being.

The memoir is deliberately personal, not a representative account of all trans men, and its access to publication, medical transition, and particular family history shape what it can claim. Some readers may also resist its movement toward forgiveness. Its feminist value lies in keeping that tension open: trans inclusion is not simply admission into existing manhood, and critique of masculinity need not deny trans men’s identities. The harder question is how men can claim embodiment and belonging while refusing the violence through which patriarchal culture certifies them.
