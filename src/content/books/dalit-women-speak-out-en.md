*Dalit Women Speak Out* grows from a multiyear study across four Indian states and brings together 500 Dalit women’s narratives of violence. It refuses to separate those experiences into unrelated categories of “domestic violence,” “sexual assault,” or “caste discrimination.” Caste, class, and gender are treated as institutions that organize power together. Harm does not arise because three identity risks happen to accumulate; Dalit women’s position in land, labor, sexuality, and public space is itself one of the means by which caste order is reproduced.

The evidence makes “endemic” more than a rhetorical description. Among respondents, verbal abuse, physical assault, sexual harassment and assault, domestic violence, and rape all appeared at substantial rates; caste epithets were frequently joined to sexual insult and threat. The point of the numbers is not to turn trauma into a league table. They reveal a continuum: everyday humiliation, blocked access to water and movement, labor exploitation, collective punishment, and sexual violence differ in severity but repeatedly enforce the place Dalit women are expected to occupy.

Sexual violence has a specific political function in this account. Attacks by dominant-caste men may retaliate against families demanding land, wages, or political rights and warn an entire community about the limits of dissent. At the same time, patriarchal harm within households and Dalit communities cannot be silenced in the name of anti-caste unity. “Caste, class and gender” is therefore not a row of labels. It makes two movements confront their blind spots: mainstream Indian feminism cannot universalize dominant-caste experience, and anti-caste politics cannot postpone gender justice until after liberation.

“Speaking out” also requires following what happens after violence. A woman who seeks a complaint may face family pressure, village boycott, economic retaliation, police refusal to register a case, inadequate medical evidence, downgraded charges, and prolonged judicial delay. Formal laws against caste discrimination do not guarantee that institutions will recognize the caste function of an attack. Where police and local officials share dominant-group prejudice, procedure becomes a second site of harm. Justice must therefore include livelihood, safe shelter, health care, witness protection, and collective organizing power, not merely conviction counts.

The book’s methodological value lies in placing women’s accounts beside institutional analysis. Testimony is not an emotional illustration for a theory formed elsewhere; it reveals causal relations that official categories miss—why an assault connects to a land dispute, why silence may be a survival strategy, or why relatives can be both supporters and sources of pressure. Yet “giving voice” should not be romanticized. Researchers select, translate, and organize five hundred lives, so readers must still ask whose voice was easiest to record and who remained silent because speaking was unsafe or stigmatized.

This regionally bounded study, published in 2011, cannot represent every Dalit woman in India or capture subsequent changes in movements, law enforcement, and digital public life. It gives limited attention to queer, trans, disabled, urban-migrant, and religiously diverse Dalit experiences, while statistics cannot contain the complexity of any life. These limits should prevent “Dalit women” from becoming another homogeneous victim category. The volume belongs alongside Dalit women’s memoirs, movement statements, and more recent regional research.

For FemRes, *Dalit Women Speak Out* changes the unit of anti-violence analysis. The question is not only how to punish an individual perpetrator, but how land relations, dependent labor, policing, courts, households, and community politics jointly produce impunity. Feminism without caste analysis misnames structural violence as cultural backwardness or private tragedy; an archive that records injury without Dalit women’s organizing, judgment, and demands speaks over them again. Voice must lead to resources, representation, and institutional accountability.
