Lois M. Smith and Alfred Padula’s *Sex and Revolution* examines how the Cuban Revolution transformed women’s education, employment, health, family law, sexuality, and political participation. Mass literacy, expanded schooling, paid work, childcare, and the 1975 Family Code altered the institutional conditions of women’s lives. The Federation of Cuban Women became a major vehicle for mobilization and service provision, demonstrating the scale at which a socialist state could make gender equality part of national development.

The book also studies the distance between official equality and intimate life. Machismo, the sexual division of domestic labor, double standards, and male authority did not disappear through legal reform. State organizations could create resources while defining which demands were legitimate and subordinating autonomous feminist politics to revolutionary unity. Women were celebrated as workers and revolutionary citizens yet continued to carry disproportionate household labor and to negotiate sexuality within strong norms of family and nation.

Written in the 1990s, the study reflects its archive and period; later research is needed on the Special Period, racial inequality, queer and trans life, migration, dissent, and policy change. Readers should also distinguish achievements under severe external pressure from apologetics for state control. The book’s lasting value is comparative: it shows both how rapidly material conditions can change when equality receives institutional investment and why production, law, and social services alone do not automatically democratize the home or political voice.
