*Trap Door* begins from a contradiction that liberal accounts of representation often evade: trans people can become more visible in art, television, journalism, and public debate while violence, policing, poverty, and legal exclusion intensify. Through essays, conversations, images, and archival dossiers, the anthology asks who is permitted to appear, under what terms, and for whose benefit. Its title names the false invitation built into recognition. A door seems to offer entry into cultural legitimacy, yet becomes a trap when trans people are welcomed only if they remain beautiful, intelligible, marketable, or compliant with dominant ideas of citizenship.

The volume's most important feminist contribution is to move representation away from a simple arithmetic of positive and negative images. Its contributors connect visual culture to racial capitalism, disability, incarceration, police violence, performance, fashion, archives, and collective survival, with particular attention to trans women of color and gender-nonconforming artists. Visibility is therefore treated as an infrastructure of power rather than an automatic good. The alternative figure of the “trapdoor”—a concealed passage neither entrance nor exit—suggests practices that do more than request inclusion: self-authored archives, fugitive aesthetics, mutual protection, and forms of cultural production that refuse the institutions seeking to classify them.

Because it is a large and deliberately heterogeneous anthology, *Trap Door* is not a linear introduction to trans studies, and its essays do not resolve every tension between artistic autonomy, institutional access, and material redistribution. Much of its immediate context is the United States art world of the 2010s, so readers should extend its framework cautiously across other media and political settings. That situatedness is also why the book remains productive. For FemRes, it offers a rigorous way to ask whether representation changes who controls housing, healthcare, work, safety, and historical memory—or merely gives an unequal system a more inclusive image.
