*The Gendered Worlds of Latin American Women Workers* places the twentieth-century factory back inside the household, neighborhood, and nation. Edited by John D. French and Daniel James, its nine studies span Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, and Guatemala and combine archival research with oral history. Their central question is deceptively simple: when a woman enters paid employment, why does she remain defined first as daughter, wife, or mother? “Worker” was never a neutral identity, and industrialization did not straightforwardly liberate women from domestic authority.

Across the collection, factories need women’s labor while representing it as temporary, unskilled, and morally dangerous. A São Paulo woman could be treated as a skilled housewife at home yet classified as an “unskilled” operative on the production line; the family-wage ideal then named men as authentic providers and rationalized women’s lower pay. Studies of Argentine meatpacking and Medellín textiles show how female virtue, family stability, and national modernization became a shared vocabulary for corporate discipline and social control.

The contributors do not reduce factory women to passive victims. Oral histories recover friendships, desire, sociability, and collective identities formed in workshops, boarding arrangements, dances, and streets. Waged work could produce income, new public space, and leverage within households, even under severe constraint. These apparently nonpolitical relations reveal that class consciousness did not spring automatically from factory employment; it was made through gender norms, emotional life, conflict, and practices of mutual aid.

The chapters on unions and movements are especially revealing. Guatemalan women experienced isolation within male-led organizations, while politics in Chilean copper communities mobilized miners’ wives as guardians of family life. Elsewhere, women used maternal and neighborhood identities to demand public resources. Attention to domestic violence and sexual control exposes the limits of labor politics that discusses wages at the factory gate but treats power at home as private: such an approach cannot explain who is able to attend meetings, strike, or remain in leadership.

The book concentrates on urban industrial labor in a particular period, giving uneven attention to rural, domestic, and informal work or to Indigenous and Afro-descendant women; platform work and today’s transnational supply chains lie beyond its scope. Those absences should prompt rather than halt inquiry into who enters the labor archive. Its lasting feminist contribution is to dismantle the factory/home and public/private divides: women workers’ citizenship is not an issue beside labor, but is produced simultaneously through wages, respectability, care, violence, and the right to organize.
