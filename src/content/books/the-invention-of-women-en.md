Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí’s starting point is not that Yoruba society was free of hierarchy, but that Western observers misdescribed how hierarchy worked. Colonial officials, missionaries, and later social scientists treated anatomical sex as the obvious foundation of social order. They then translated Yoruba institutions through imported categories such as man, woman, wife, and patriarchy. The result was not neutral description: it made a Western ‘bio-logic’ appear universal and turned a historically specific gender system into the measure of every society.

Against this framework, Oyěwùmí emphasizes seniority, lineage position, and changing social relations. In her account, many Yoruba terms do not encode sex in the way their English translations suggest, while authority could follow age and social location rather than a fixed male/female opposition. Colonial rule hardened distinctions by recognizing men as household heads, political representatives, and modern subjects. Gender was therefore not simply discovered in Yorubaland; it was partly produced through administration, translation, Christianity, and the disciplines that claimed to study African life.

The book’s feminist importance lies in its challenge to conceptual imperialism. If ‘woman’ is assumed to name the same political subject everywhere, feminism can reproduce colonial knowledge even while seeking liberation. Oyěwùmí asks who created the categories, what language makes them seem natural, and which institutions gain power when they are imposed. This does not require abandoning analysis of sexual violence or reproductive inequality. It requires showing how those harms are organized in a specific history rather than treating Western gender theory as a ready-made grid.

The argument has also generated serious debate. Critics have questioned whether the book underestimates embodied difference, sexual division, and evidence of gendered power before colonialism, or presents Yoruba social life as more coherent than it was. Those objections matter because decolonization should not become a romantic claim that patriarchy arrived wholly from outside. The most productive reading holds both insights together: colonialism transformed and intensified social categories, while local forms of power still require feminist scrutiny.

For FemRes readers, The Invention of Women is best approached as a methodological intervention. It changes the questions asked of archives, translations, kinship, and political vocabulary. Rather than adding an ‘African example’ to a theory built elsewhere, it asks whether the theory itself survives contact with Yoruba concepts. That reversal makes the book indispensable to African, decolonial, and transnational feminist study.
