*The Transgender Studies Reader*, edited by Susan Stryker and Stephen Whittle, assembles fifty texts that document how transgender studies emerged from—and argued with—sexology, medicine, feminism, queer theory, history, sociology, law, and activist knowledge. Early medico-legal classifications appear beside trans autobiographical and political writing, feminist attacks and rebuttals, theories of gender performance, accounts of embodiment, and movement documents. This architecture makes the volume more than a celebration of a new discipline: it records the struggle over who had the authority to describe gender variance and how pathologized subjects became producers of theory.

Stryker’s framing of “desubjugated knowledges” captures the reader’s feminist importance. Medical and academic archives cannot simply be discarded, because they shaped real lives and contain histories that can be read against institutional intentions. Nor can hostile feminist texts be forgotten if their effects and trans responses are to be understood. By placing contradictory sources together, the collection reveals knowledge as a field of power. Trans studies becomes possible when first-person experience, community memory, and activism are treated not merely as data for experts but as intellectual work capable of revising the categories of body, identity, desire, and sex.

The canon is strongly Anglophone and Euro-American, and a 2006 reader inevitably reflects terminology and debates that later scholarship has complicated through race, coloniality, disability, migration, and political economy. Some selections require careful contextualization because inclusion documents influence rather than endorsement. Those limits make the companion second reader essential. For FemRes, the first volume remains foundational as a history of a field constructing itself from contested archives. It teaches readers to ask not only what a text says about trans people, but who is permitted to speak, which institutions preserve speech, and how subordinated knowledge can reorganize feminism itself.
