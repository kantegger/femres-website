*Coming Out as Dalit* braids a coming-of-age story, family memory, movement history, and reporting to explain how a hierarchy legally abolished can persist in voices, surnames, schools, and desire. Yashica Dutt was born into a Dalit family historically assigned to manual scavenging. Hoping her children could evade discrimination, her mother taught them to present as upper caste. Learning standard English, suppressing a regional accent, curating acceptable biographical details, and even trying to lighten her skin were not simple vanity; they formed a survival curriculum for entry into educational and middle-class spaces.

Concealment in this account does not make caste disappear. It transfers to the subordinated person the entire labor of sustaining the fiction of castelessness. Dutt continually calculates what friends know, what to write on a form, which family histories may be told, and whether an incidental question will expose her. The security obtained by “passing” carries fear of discovery, estrangement from family memory, and guilt toward ancestors whose struggle made her mobility possible. The memoir recasts upward mobility as an unequal bargain: institutions need not admit their bias if they reward those most able to hide its marks.

In 2016, Rohith Vemula, a Dalit doctoral student at the University of Hyderabad, died by suicide after institutional exclusion; the words he left pushed Dutt to acknowledge publicly the identity she had long concealed. This is not staged as an instant completion of self-liberation but as the beginning of reconnecting private shame to collective history. She created an online archive of Dalit discrimination and confronted the fact that “coming out” meant more than naming a category. It involved admitting how safety and ambition had distanced her from a community and accepting new scrutiny, abuse, and pressure to represent it.

The memoir then widens into an accessible history for readers new to caste. Dutt discusses untouchability, Ambedkar’s political legacy, reservation, and the barriers to Dalit participation in education and cultural production. She particularly contests the story that reservation is an unfair concession. When schooling, examination language, family capital, hiring networks, and everyday dignity are unequally distributed, a common score presented as merit merely launders inherited advantage into personal achievement. Her experience in elite schools and journalism brings caste out of the imagined distance of rural atrocity.

Feminism is an indispensable strand. Caste governs women’s sexuality and marriage through endogamy, lineage, and purity, while Dalit women face labor extraction, sexual violence, and dominant-caste scrutiny; mainstream Indian feminism and media have nevertheless often lacked Dalit voices. Dutt introduces Dalit women’s movements and shows that a woman concealing caste manages more than a résumé. Skin color, accent, respectability, romance, and the family relations disclosed by marriage all become evidence to control. Visibility is not abstract self-expression but a risk priced by gender and class.

The title’s borrowing from queer “coming out” created a widely circulating vocabulary but also requires care. Caste is read through birth, surname, community networks, and histories of labor; queer identity has distinct histories of desire, family danger, and state regulation. A shared concealment–disclosure pattern does not make them equivalent. Queer and Dalit commentators have questioned whether the borrowing obscures the movement history of queer language. A responsible reading treats the metaphor as a limited tool for secrecy, stigma, and narrative agency while giving people at the Dalit–queer intersection particular authority to define its boundaries.

This entry describes the 2019 South Asian edition. The expanded 2024 U.S. edition adds chapters on technology, the model-minority myth, and caste across borders; those later materials should not be retroactively attributed to the first book. Even within its original scope, Dutt’s route cannot stand for every Dalit life. English education, elite universities, international journalism, and some opportunity to pass distinguish her from people whose occupation, region, or bodies are made impossible to conceal. Its public-facing history should be deepened with regional scholarship, labor studies, and more Dalit women’s writing.

Do not read public disclosure merely as an inspirational climax. Trace instead how each institution makes hiding seem necessary and who bears the consequences of visibility. Pair the memoir with *Caste Matters* on elite institutions and cultural capital, *The Prisons We Broke* on collective memory, *Against the Madness of Manu* on endogamy, and specific research on reservation and workplace discrimination. Its crucial question for FemRes is no longer “Why did she not tell people sooner?” but what kind of society makes erasing one’s history an admission price for education, work, love, and safety.
