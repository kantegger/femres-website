*Arrested Justice* asks more than how much violence Black women endure. It examines how the American “prison nation” decides which violence becomes legible, which victims merit protection, and who is drawn into punishment when she seeks help. Drawing on more than two decades of anti-violence research and organizing, Beth E. Richie connects battering, rape, stalking, incest, and sexual harassment in marginalized communities to punitive policy, welfare retrenchment, racialized policing, and mass incarceration. Her central claim is that Black women are simultaneously underprotected and overpunished—and that these are not separate policy failures but complementary effects of the same political order.

Richie first reconstructs the US anti-violence movement’s passage from radical social critique into the institutional mainstream. Shelters, rape-crisis centers, and domestic-violence laws were crucial victories. Yet when government recognition, prosecution, and harsher sentencing became dominant measures of success, the movement’s analysis of patriarchy, capitalism, racism, and heterosexism narrowed. “Winning the mainstream” could therefore mean losing a movement: survivors whose stories fit official expectations gained visibility, while poor Black women’s more complicated circumstances were stripped of context and recoded as noncompliance, unreliability, or criminal risk.

The book’s most powerful analytic device is the “male violence matrix.” Violence within that matrix includes not only bodily harm by an intimate partner but sexual and emotional domination in communities and the injuries produced by police, courts, prisons, and service agencies. These personal, community, and state levels are mutually reinforcing rather than merely adjacent. A woman may call police to escape a partner and then be criminalized for self-defense, a drug offense, a child-custody dispute, or conduct associated with poverty. Richie thereby breaks apart the innocent-victim/dangerous-offender binary and shows how one person can be survivor, caregiver, and defendant at once.

Her method combines Black feminist theory, policy history, and testimony from women whose cases have largely disappeared from public memory. These narratives do not function as emotional decoration; they test the categories through which institutions produce knowledge. Data limited to reports, convictions, or shelter use will miss people who avoid the system because they distrust police, fear losing their children, anticipate racist punishment of a partner, or risk arrest themselves. Beginning from the margins makes relations of power visible that gender-neutral policy conceals, and turns the authority to define “credible” violence into a feminist question in its own right.

Richie’s critique of carceral feminism is therefore not an argument for passivity in the face of harm. It targets the assumption that police, prosecution, and prison should be the default response, when those institutions often import state violence into already precarious lives. Severe sentences may perform public condemnation without supplying housing, income, health care, childcare, or long-term accompaniment; for marginalized women, they may intensify family separation and drain community resources. A serious account of safety must ask whether an intervention expands a survivor’s autonomy and reduces coerced contact with punishment, not simply whether it produces an arrest.

Nor does the book romanticize “community” as an automatically safe alternative. Families and neighborhoods can protect male authority, suppress disclosure, or demand that Black women remain silent in the name of racial solidarity. Richie instead points toward Black women-led organizing, cross-movement coalitions, and material support infrastructures that treat housing, health care, income, legal assistance, youth work, and accountability as the foundations of safety. This double refusal—of patriarchal interpersonal violence and racist state violence—makes abolition a project of building social capacity, not a single act of removing police.

The book’s limits are also real. Published in 2012, it is rooted in a specific history of US Black communities, welfare transformation, and “tough on crime” politics; its cases and policy landscape require updating through the work of Say Her Name, Black trans organizing, and the abolitionist mobilizations after 2020. Its focus on Black women is its great analytic strength, but readers will need additional work for fuller accounts of Indigenous, immigrant, disabled, and transgender experiences. Those boundaries do not invalidate the matrix; they require that it be reconstructed rather than mechanically exported in each new setting.

A productive reading path follows three threads: the anti-violence movement’s mainstreaming, the matrix linking private injury to public power, and the concluding Black feminist strategies for change. Read it next to *Invisible No More* to see how policing itself is gendered, then with *The Color of Violence* and *Abolition. Feminism. Now.* to place Richie’s argument in a wider history of women-of-color organizing. Its enduring value for FemRes is not a slogan against incarceration but an instrument of judgment: every policy proposed in the name of women’s safety must reveal whose life-sustaining resources it expands and whom it moves closer to punishment.
