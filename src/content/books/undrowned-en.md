Alexis Pauline Gumbs describes *Undrowned* as a meditation for social movements guided by marine mammals. Across brief, incantatory chapters, whales, dolphins, seals, and other aquatic relatives prompt practices of breathing, listening, echolocation, migration, conflict, rest, and collective protection. The ocean is not a peaceful realm outside politics: it bears the violence of slavery, settler colonialism, extraction, military sonar, captivity, and climate disruption. By bringing naturalist observation into conversation with Black feminist thought, Gumbs asks how people struggling to survive hostile conditions might change not only their tactics but their senses and relations.

This is ecofeminism without the old equation of women with a passive, nurturing nature. Marine mammals appear as queer, intelligent, social beings shaped by danger and adaptation, while Black feminist survival becomes more than individual resilience. Breath is bodily and political; listening can displace the fantasy that vision and visibility are the only routes to knowledge; interdependence becomes a practice rather than a sentimental slogan. Gumbs also places ancestral memory beside species history, linking the endangered North Atlantic right whale to histories of Indigenous dispossession and enslavement. The method is poetic and devotional, but its purpose is material: to rehearse abolitionist, anti-extractive ways of being alive together.

Readers should not mistake *Undrowned* for marine biology or treat animal behavior as a ready-made political program. Metaphor can appropriate other species just as easily as it can challenge human supremacy, and the book's exercises will feel more generative to some readers than to others. Gumbs acknowledges this risk by offering questions rather than claiming mastery over marine life. For FemRes, the book is valuable precisely as an experiment in method: it tests whether feminist theory can learn across species without turning difference into property, and whether movements can practice care, adaptation, and freedom before the world they seek has fully arrived.
