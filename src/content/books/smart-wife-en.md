*The Smart Wife* addresses not one device but a family of technologies assigned the functions of a wife. Siri, Alexa, and Google Home answer and remind; robotic vacuums maintain order; care devices monitor relatives; virtual companions such as Hikari Azuma supply emotional attention; sex robots extend availability into intimacy. Yolande Strengers and Jenny Kennedy call these activities “wifework” and ask whether the future home has abolished gendered labor or merely hidden the idealized wife inside its devices.

Their dominant prototype is the fantasy of a white, middle-class, heterosexual 1950s housewife: nurturing, organized, permanently available, capable of meeting everyone’s needs while expressing none of her own. Voice assistants carry feminine names and voices, perform friendliness or flirtation, and have often responded submissively to abuse; domestic devices promise a frictionless home in which no one must negotiate who cleans. What appears to be a naturally usable personality is a historical script. Male-dominated technology industries package a particular racial and class position as universal service.

Delegating wifework to machines does not mean household labor disappears. Smart homes require purchasing, installation, connection, updating, troubleshooting, data interpretation, and coordination among residents, producing new “digital housework.” A robotic vacuum still needs obstacles cleared; a care system still needs someone to interpret and answer an alert. Who researches products, remembers passwords, maintains rules, and ultimately responds to children, elders, or partners is rarely represented in the interface. Convenience can redistribute tasks while returning managerial labor to the person already responsible for the home.

Feminization also trains users in how to address a voice understood as female. Corporate scripts have turned sexual harassment and insults into flirtation, apology, or coy deflection, making submission an everyday interaction. Adding a masculine voice cannot by itself solve the problem because the deeper association binds service, disposability, and femininity. The seamless assistant also obscures customer-service staff, data labelers, cleaners, and carers behind the system. The virtual wife’s inexhaustible patience can conceal the conditions of actual women’s work.

The smart home is also a site of surveillance and unequal power. Devices collect speech, routines, energy use, health information, and intimate behavior, while administrator privileges determine who can inspect records or control locks and temperature. Even in equitable households, this raises questions of consent and privacy. In coercive or violent relationships, convenience functions can become tools of monitoring and control. A product designed only for a harmonious heterosexual nuclear family misses that household members do not necessarily share interests, and that tenants, children, carers, and survivors face different risks.

Strengers and Kennedy do not end by making every feminized technology a neutral grey box. Their Smart Wife “manifesta” asks for something more complex: stop devaluing care, emotion, and domestic competence because they are coded feminine, while designing a greater diversity of genders, bodies, households, and voices. Devices should encourage shared responsibility rather than offer the wealthiest user an endlessly compliant servant. A feminist reboot must change representation and also who designs, who controls data, who performs maintenance, and whose life is treated as default.

The analysis has limits. It primarily observes affluent consumer markets and English-language smart homes, with less sustained attention to hardware supply chains, data-labeling labor, energy costs, or the different care needs of low-income households. Products and scripted responses change rapidly, making individual examples perishable. “Wife” as an analytic figure can also leave nonmarital households and queer care networks at the edge. The book remains valuable not as a permanent scorecard for devices but as a set of questions that can travel as technology changes.

Choose one device at home and list the task it claims to replace, maintenance it creates, data it gathers, and person who holds control. Then ask how those distributions intersect with gender, class, age, disability, and household status. Work on care economies, technology-facilitated abuse, and global digital labor can supplement the consumer-side account. The book’s strongest reminder is that technology will not automatically liberate a household from patriarchy. “Smart” can mean freer life only when care is shared, labor recognized, and refusal and privacy designed as capabilities rather than inconveniences.
