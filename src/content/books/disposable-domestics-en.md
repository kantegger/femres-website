Grace Chang’s title names the governing contradiction of immigrant women’s labor in the United States: indispensable enough to clean homes and offices, raise children, support disabled and older people, and staff service economies, yet “disposable” when wages, legal status, welfare, or political belonging are at stake. First published in 2000, the book’s 2016 edition adds a foreword by Alicia Garza and an afterword by Ai-jen Poo, placing the original analysis beside newer racial-justice and domestic-worker organizing.

Chang rejects the familiar story in which migration begins with an isolated individual choosing opportunity abroad. She traces how debt, trade policy, structural adjustment, and unequal development disrupt livelihoods in sending countries, while employers and affluent economies demand low-cost labor. Movement across a border is therefore produced at both ends: global policy helps displace workers, then immigration policy makes their labor more exploitable after arrival.

The United States can consequently describe immigrants as a fiscal burden while extracting the work that makes households, hospitals, hotels, and cities function. Chang reads immigrant exclusion, welfare retrenchment, and labor-market segmentation together. The contradiction is useful to power: workers are visible enough to blame during elections, but kept legally and socially invisible when they demand benefits, safety, education, or a living wage.

Domestic and care labor are especially vulnerable because they occur in spaces imagined as private and because their skills have long been naturalized as women’s duty. Histories of racialized service and exclusions from ordinary labor protections make the low wage look like a property of the worker rather than a policy outcome. The category “unskilled” conceals judgment, bodily effort, emotional regulation, and responsibility for another person’s survival.

This analysis also complicates a celebratory account of women entering professional work. When public services shrink and men do not take an equal share of reproduction, some better-paid women solve their own care deficit by purchasing the labor of poorer migrant women. Gender equality for one household can thus rest on a hierarchy of race, citizenship, and class unless care is redistributed across men, employers, and the state rather than merely transferred between women.

Chang refuses to portray immigrant workers only as evidence of suffering. Their organizing, refusal, mutual support, and public testimony challenge the very condition of disposability. The additions by Garza and Poo make this political thread especially visible: struggles over domestic work belong with movements against racism, deportability, and economic abandonment, not in a separate charitable category for “helping” vulnerable women.

The book’s core research reflects the United States at the turn of the century, even though the 2016 edition demonstrates its continued relevance. Laws, migration routes, worker organizations, and care markets have changed, and its large structural frame cannot substitute for detailed accounts of status, nationality, disability, sexuality, or occupation. Readers should also resist making immigrant women synonymous with care work; that association is itself a product of the labor regime under examination.

Read *Disposable Domestics* by testing a public claim that immigrants “take” resources against the full balance sheet of labor, taxes, remittances, denied benefits, and subsidized care. Map the chain from international economic policy to visa status, workplace rules, and household convenience, then add the demands of worker-led organizations. The feminist question is not how to make essential workers slightly less disposable, but how to end a system that requires anyone’s disposability to provide care.
