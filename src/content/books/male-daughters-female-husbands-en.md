Based on historical and ethnographic research in Nnobi, an Igbo community in southeastern Nigeria, Ifi Amadiume demonstrates that social positions commonly translated as ‘male’ or ‘female’ were not always attached to bodies in a fixed way. A woman could become a ‘male daughter’ in order to retain lineage property, or a ‘female husband’ who married a wife and exercised recognized economic and political authority. These arrangements were not modern identities waiting to be named; they were institutional roles embedded in kinship, inheritance, motherhood, and collective obligation.

Amadiume’s intervention is sharper than the claim that precolonial Africa simply accepted gender fluidity. She shows that the analytic fusion of sex, gender, and social power obscures systems organized through multiple principles. Motherhood could be a source of public authority, women’s organizations could regulate markets and sanctions, and wealth or title could alter a person’s position. The category ‘husband’ described a relationship to labor, property, and lineage rather than automatically designating a man or a particular sexuality.

Colonial administration disrupted this flexibility. Officials searched for male chiefs and household heads, codified custom through patriarchal assumptions, and redirected land, cash crops, education, and formal politics toward men. Missionary morality also recast indigenous arrangements as deviant or unintelligible. The book thus explains how colonialism did more than suppress women; it changed the grammar through which authority and difference could be recognized.

For feminist and queer theory, the work remains generative because it refuses to let Euro-American categories stand as universal history. At the same time, it should not be used to claim that Nnobi society lacked hierarchy, coercion, or sexual difference. Social mobility was structured by lineage, wealth, reproduction, and status, and the available roles were not equivalent to contemporary self-defined identities. Its value lies in disciplined comparison, not in turning Africa into evidence for a present-day slogan.

Read alongside Oyěwùmí, Male Daughters, Female Husbands becomes part of a larger decolonial argument about knowledge. Translation can manufacture binaries; archives can record only the authorities colonial government chose to recognize; and feminist concepts must remain answerable to the societies they describe. The book gives readers a concrete case through which to rethink gender as an institution rather than an unquestioned property of bodies.
