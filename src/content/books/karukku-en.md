*Karukku* begins from the life of a Tamil Dalit Christian woman and breaks the comforting fiction that religious equality automatically transcends caste. Bama writes public humiliations in childhood, segregation at school, village labor, and her eventual disillusionment as a nun: a church preaching love nonetheless reproduces caste through work, education, and everyday contact. The title evokes the double-edged, serrated palmyra leaf—both the cuts oppression leaves and writing’s capacity to cut back through silence.

Its form is itself political. This is not a smooth autobiography ordered by trauma and eventual success, but a collage of episodes, memory, speech, and community rhythm that joins an individual life to collective history. Education helps the narrator recognize power and leave the convent, yet may also demand distance from her language and people. Self-recovery is therefore not an exceptional individual escaping poverty; it means living through anger, shame, conflict over faith, and responsibility to community without surrendering any of them.

The book’s feminist force lies in locating caste, religious patriarchy, and labor on the same body. Dalit women confront dominant-caste violence while also negotiating church hierarchy, family authority, and male-led politics. *Karukku* does not represent every Dalit woman, and its Catholic and Tamil specificity must not be flattened; translation cannot fully carry its dialect, wordplay, and sound. That limit is precisely why it should be read as situated Dalit feminist literature rather than universal suffering packaged for a mainstream audience.
