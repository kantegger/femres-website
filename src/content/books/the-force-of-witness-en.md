Rosa-Linda Fregoso asks what witnessing can do when the institutions charged with recognizing violence are themselves implicated in impunity. Moving through Mexico’s contra-feminicide movement, she rejects the image of the witness as a detached individual who simply reports facts to a court. Witnessing becomes collective, relational, and political: a practice for preserving lives and claims that official archives would otherwise erase.

The book follows activist mothers who carry the memory of murdered and disappeared daughters, expert witnesses in legal cases, artists and filmmakers who construct counter-archives, and communities that make grief publicly consequential. Fregoso also includes her own position as scholar, activist, citizen, and survivor. That reflexivity matters because it refuses the fiction that research on violence can be ethically neutral or untouched by unequal relations of looking.

Her analysis connects intimate misogyny to a wider necropolitical order: state abandonment, racial capitalism, colonial conquest, border regimes, and institutions that distribute vulnerability. “Contra feminicide” therefore names more than opposition to individual killings. It describes practices that challenge the social conditions under which feminized and racialized people are rendered disposable and their families are forced to become investigators.

Fregoso is especially attentive to art’s double edge. Images and testimony can mobilize solidarity and contest denial, but they can also turn dead women into endlessly circulating spectacles. Ethical witnessing requires presence, accountability, and attention to the wishes and political work of survivors and relatives. The viewer is asked not merely to feel, but to enter a relation of responsibility.

This book deepens the framework developed in earlier feminicide scholarship by centering the labor of response rather than only the machinery of death. It is demanding but highly useful for readers working across feminist theory, visual culture, law, memory studies, and anti-violence organizing. Its lasting question is not whether we have seen enough evidence, but what forms of collective action our seeing obliges.
