In *Nimo’s War, Emma’s War*, Cynthia Enloe narrates the US invasion and occupation of Iraq through eight women—four Iraqi and four American—rather than through generals, elections, or weapons systems. Their lives include displacement, military service, antiwar organizing, marriage, paid work, and the daily effort to keep families functioning. The paired title resists a single national “women’s experience”: Nimo and Emma inhabit radically unequal positions within the same war, connected by imperial policy without becoming equivalent.

Enloe’s feminist curiosity follows the decisions official history treats as background. Who can travel safely, who loses employment, whose care work compensates for a damaged state, which military wife must normalize deployment, and which Iraqi woman becomes legible to Western media? These intimate arrangements are not consequences that occur after strategy. They are the infrastructure through which occupation operates and through which militarized ideas of protection, sacrifice, and respectable femininity acquire consent.

The book’s biographical approach creates proximity but cannot make eight lives representative of two countries, and the author’s access and position shape what can be known. Its purpose is not to replace structural analysis with personal stories. It demonstrates that strategy is incomplete unless it can account for the unequal domestic and emotional worlds it produces. For FemRes, the book offers a disciplined way to read war from below while refusing both the rescue fantasy about Iraqi women and the erasure of American women’s participation in militarism.
