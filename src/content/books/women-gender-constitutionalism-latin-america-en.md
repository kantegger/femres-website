*Women, Gender, and Constitutionalism in Latin America* compares how constitutional design, legislation, and judicial practice have addressed the subordination of women and LGBTQIA+ people across eleven jurisdictions. Its country studies range from Costa Rica, Colombia, Ecuador, and Bolivia to Brazil, Mexico, Peru, Chile, Puerto Rico, Argentina, and Uruguay. Rather than treating progressive constitutional language as self-executing, the contributors place doctrine within political history, feminist and queer mobilization, colonial legacies, institutional capacity, and conflicts over policy. The result is a regional map of both innovation and uneven enforcement.

The volume's central feminist question is whether constitutionalism can dismantle the old division between a public sphere of equal citizens and a private sphere governed by family hierarchy and heterosexual norms. Courts and constitutions may recognize equality, reproductive autonomy, same-sex relationships, or protection from violence while preserving omissions and ambivalent reasoning that limit those rights in practice. Comparison makes visible patterns that a single landmark judgment can hide: which movements gain access to constitutional language, whose injuries become rights claims, and how formal victories interact with administrative systems, economic inequality, and political backlash.

This is a specialist edited collection, not a general history of Latin American feminism, and legal categories can never substitute for the experiences of Indigenous, Afro-descendant, rural, migrant, disabled, poor, queer, and trans communities. Constitutional law also changes quickly; readers should verify later decisions and reforms rather than treating the 2024 survey as permanently current. Its value lies in teaching a method of critical comparison. For FemRes, the book shows why constitutions matter without turning them into magical texts: gender justice depends on movements that create legal possibilities, institutions capable of realizing them, and political struggle over whose life the constitutional order is designed to protect.
