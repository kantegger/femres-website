Susan Stryker’s *Transgender History* offers an accessible chronology of modern trans life and organizing in the United States, moving from postwar transsexual and transvestite networks through the radical politics of the late 1960s and early 1970s, identity-based movements after the mid-1970s, and the conflicts and visibility of the 1990s and 2000s. Sidebars, short biographies, primary-text excerpts, and discussions of popular culture connect terminology to people and institutions. The book makes clear that contemporary trans politics did not appear suddenly through social media or elite recognition; it was built through publications, clinics, street resistance, mutual support, legal struggle, and contested alliances.

This history changes feminist and LGBTQ+ narratives that treat trans people as late arrivals. It recovers organizing around policing, poverty, healthcare, incarceration, and public space while tracking conflicts with medical authorities, gay and lesbian institutions, and exclusionary feminisms. Stryker also shows that names and categories change. “Transgender” can enable coalition, but it does not erase differences among drag performers, transsexual people, gender-nonconforming communities, and others whose lives may not fit current identities. History therefore becomes a safeguard against using today’s vocabulary as if it were timeless.

Its clarity requires compression, and its national focus leaves global histories, Indigenous gender systems, and many racial and class differences needing fuller treatment elsewhere. A chronological survey can also make discontinuous struggles look more unified in retrospect than they felt to participants. These are reasons to use the book as an orientation rather than a complete canon. For FemRes readers, it supplies the movement memory necessary to recognize recurring battles over care, legitimacy, policing, and coalition. It replaces the myth of trans novelty with a history of organized survival and makes present rights legible as unfinished achievements rather than gifts from institutions.
