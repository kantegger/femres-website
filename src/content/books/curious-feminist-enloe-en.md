*The Curious Feminist* turns curiosity from a personality trait into a method of political inquiry. Cynthia Enloe asks readers to repeat deceptively simple questions: Where are the women? Who is treated as a serious political actor? Who benefits from their labor, silence, or invisibility? Those questions redraw the conventional map of international relations. States, armies, corporations, and diplomats cease to be its only protagonists; Asian sneaker workers, military wives, sex workers, daughters, refugees, peace activists, and junior administrators emerge as conditions on which global politics depends.

The opening section enters globalization through a “globetrotting sneaker.” Brands, contractors, and trade policy are usually described as market mechanisms, but Enloe follows young women’s wages, dormitories, family expectations, and the managed image of feminine docility. Low cost is not a natural competitive advantage. It depends on corporations, governments, and households jointly producing a replaceable, underpaid workforce that is difficult to organize. Centering these workers does not merely add women’s examples to an existing model; it changes the explanation of capital, development, and supply-chain power.

“Wars are never over there” forms a second line. Nationalism manufactures masculine protectors and feminine victims, while governments mobilize particular masculinities to recruit soldiers, fashion presidential authority, and distinguish courage from betrayal. Armies also depend on women for services around bases, family emotion, daily care, and legitimacy. Enloe therefore studies femininities and masculinities together. Militarization is not just a growing troop count; it is the reorganization of social relations so that force, obedience, sacrifice, and “toughness” appear normal.

The postwar essays are especially valuable because a ceasefire can be mistaken for the restoration of gendered normality. Discussions of Vietnam, Afghanistan, and Iraq show demobilization pushing women out of wartime employment, new security institutions reproducing male networks, and international aid reducing women to traumatized victims. Genuine demilitarization must ask what happens to budgets, land, work, justice, and domestic violence. It must also test whether women in peacebuilding acquire institutional authority rather than merely appearing in conference photographs.

Enloe’s feminist curiosity also rejects disciplinary arrogance. Clothing, marriage, gossip, consumption, emotion, and household arrangements, dismissed as trivial, are often where power hides most effectively. Researchers who read only official documents treat the gendered work maintaining institutions as background. Enloe further asks feminists to permit surprise: if every investigation confirms what the analyst already believed, she may not have listened. Curiosity is thus both an epistemological instrument and an ethic of accountability for one’s own privilege and omissions.

Published in 2004, the collection bears the clear imprint of US-led globalization and the opening years of the Afghanistan and Iraq wars. Much remains incisive, but its organization around “women” and “men” should be read with later queer, trans, and nonbinary security studies. Searching for marginal women from the center of US empire must also meet decolonial questions about standpoint and representation. Enloe’s case-driven essays teach readers how to discover problems; they do not supply a complete political economy or a comprehensive history of every conflict discussed.

For FemRes, the book’s most durable contribution is a portable sequence of questions. Faced with a diplomatic agreement, company, war, or reconstruction program, list the wives, workers, mothers, victims, and masculine ideals presented as natural; then ask who labors to sustain those roles, who receives security, and who is excluded from decision-making. Feminist curiosity does not simply insert women into world politics. It demonstrates that world politics has not been adequately explained when gendered everyday life remains invisible.
