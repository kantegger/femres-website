Published in 1993, Ecofeminism is not a gentle argument that women are naturally closer to nature. It is Maria Mies and Vandana Shiva's joint indictment of the emerging post–Cold War global order. They place women's bodies, unpaid care, colonized resources, and nonhuman nature within one political economy, arguing that modern capitalism expands by declaring certain lives and forms of labor freely available and infinitely expendable “outside” its accounts. Ecological destruction and gender oppression are therefore not parallel concerns but connected outcomes of accumulation.

Mies focuses on “catching-up development” and housewifization. The consumption of affluent societies cannot be universally replicated because it rests upon cheap labor, land, and materials in the global South, as well as naturalized labor within households. Modernization presents waged employment as productive value while treating childbirth, caregiving, cooking, cleaning, and community maintenance as nonproductive. Capitalism, in this account, did not simply leave patriarchal households behind; it uses them to conceal the cost of reproducing workers and everyday life.

Shiva approaches the argument through agriculture, biodiversity, and the politics of knowledge. Modern science, she contends, often reduces complex ecosystems to resources that can be measured, patented, and sold. Farmers' seed saving, women's management of diverse crops, and local ecological knowledge are dismissed as backward, only to become sources of profit when biotechnology companies acquire property rights over seeds and genes. The book's discussions of GATT, the Green Revolution, and biotechnology recast “innovation” as a struggle over ownership, risk, and epistemic authority.

The authors also place reproductive and agricultural technologies in conversation. Whether the object is a woman's body or a seed, technical systems can disassemble relations capable of regenerating life into manageable components governed by experts, markets, and law. The point need not be that technology is inherently malign. The feminist questions are who establishes its purpose, who owns its products, who bears irreversible risk, and whether those whose lives are reorganized retain the power to refuse. That framework remains relevant to assisted reproduction, gene editing, data-driven farming, and climate engineering.

As an alternative, Mies and Shiva propose a “subsistence perspective”: economies should begin with the actual work of sustaining life, acknowledge ecological limits and interdependence, and value local autonomy and reciprocity rather than endless commodity growth. Subsistence here need not celebrate deprivation or confine women to the household. It names a refusal to treat care, food, and ecological reproduction as inexhaustible subsidies. Movements such as Chipko matter because participants understood forests as foundations of collective life, not inventories of timber awaiting extraction.

The classic's power comes with serious limitations. Its large oppositions between woman and nature or North and South sometimes turn women of the global South into a unified, inherently ecological subject, obscuring differences produced by caste, class, ethnicity, sexuality, trans experience, and disability. Local communities and traditions are not automatically just. Romanticizing women's subsistence labor can saddle them once again with unpaid responsibility for repairing damage capitalism created. Later feminist political ecology and Dalit, Indigenous, queer, and trans ecological thought are essential correctives to this tendency toward essentialism.

Ecofeminism is therefore best read today as a contested but still productive historical intervention. Its enduring contribution is to make readers account simultaneously for resource extraction, care labor, and colonial relations behind a commodity, and to ask whether green development merely transfers costs to less visible people. It offers no universal program ready for application. It does, however, sharpen a crucial feminist test: environmental politics that leaves ownership of land and technology, authority over knowledge, and the distribution of reproductive labor unchanged can reproduce patriarchal capitalism under a greener name.
