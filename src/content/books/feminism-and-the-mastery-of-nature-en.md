Feminism and the Mastery of Nature addresses a persistent ecofeminist dilemma. Because Western culture has aligned women with “nature,” should feminism reject that association or reclaim it positively? Val Plumwood argues that either simple answer can remain inside the master's frame. The problem is neither women's supposed lack of reason nor their supposedly innate gift for caring for Earth. It is a philosophical tradition that constructs the rational subject as a master independent of body, dependency, and nature.

Plumwood calls the structure a dualistic “logic of colonization.” A dualism is not an ordinary distinction but a hierarchy produced through recurring operations: backgrounding the subordinate's contribution, radically separating the two sides, defining the other only in relation to the center, instrumentalizing it, and homogenizing its internal differences. Human/nature, man/woman, reason/emotion, and civilized/primitive reinforce one another until the center appears self-sufficient and the other becomes a silent, replaceable resource.

The book follows the formation of this master identity through Plato, Descartes, and mechanistic philosophy. Reason becomes the capacity to rise above body, mortality, and ecological limit, while body and nature become domains requiring control. This image of the self depends upon denied dependency: the master fails to see the food, care, labor, and ecosystems that continuously sustain him. Women, colonized peoples, workers, and nonhuman life are pushed into that background, although Plumwood analyzes a shared form of legitimation rather than claiming their situations are identical.

She also challenges some apparently anti-anthropocentric alternatives. When deep ecology dissolves individual difference into an expanded ecological Self, nature may receive value only as an extension of the human self. When ecofeminism praises women as natural guardians, it can stabilize patriarchal divisions of labor. Plumwood instead develops an ethic that acknowledges relationship without swallowing difference: humans depend on others, but others are not therefore human functions; nature has agency, boundaries, and forms of existence that do not answer to our purposes.

This conceptual account remains concrete in contemporary environmental politics. Fossil economies background the atmosphere as a free waste sink, industrial agriculture treats soil and animals as inputs, and platform economies hide care workers and mineral supply chains behind polished devices. Green technology that continues to obscure mining, reproductive labor, and Indigenous land changes the instrument without changing the master story. Plumwood's five operations can serve as a practical audit of policy and design.

The book also has limits. Its argument works primarily through the Western philosophical canon and gives less sustained attention to particular colonial histories, racial capitalism, Indigenous governance, or environmental movements in the global South. Connections between “women” and other subordinated groups can remain analogical rather than historical. Later Black, Indigenous, decolonial, queer, and disability ecologies make those relations more specific and show that undoing dualism requires redistribution of land, resources, and political power as well as conceptual change.

Plumwood's enduring achievement is nevertheless to free feminism from choosing between transcending nature like the master and returning to nature as women. She asks readers to redefine reason, freedom, and subjectivity: freedom is not independence from all reliance, but the capacity to inhabit dependency without domination. For FemRes readers, the book is an exercise in conceptual detection, exposing whom every supposedly universal, self-sufficient, and progressive system has placed in its invisible background.
