Leila Ahmed’s study begins by refusing the premise that “Islam” has produced one unchanging condition for women. She moves from pre-Islamic Mediterranean and Middle Eastern gender systems through the early Muslim community, Abbasid legal culture, and modern Egypt. At each stage, religious texts coexist with political institutions, class arrangements, slavery, family economies, and inherited regional customs. The historical method matters because what later appears as sacred inevitability often emerged through conflict and interpretation.

One of the book’s most influential distinctions is between an ethical voice in Islam that affirms spiritual equality and a hierarchical legal-social order consolidated under later empires. Ahmed does not claim that scripture is simply emancipatory while history corrupts it. Instead, she shows how competing possibilities within a tradition acquire authority through particular institutions. Gender hierarchy is therefore neither external to Islamic history nor its only imaginable outcome.

The modern chapters examine Egypt, where colonial rule transformed the “woman question” into a measure of civilization. British officials could condemn the veil while administering an empire that denied political freedom, and nationalist men could defend cultural authenticity while continuing to regulate women’s lives. Ahmed’s analysis of colonial feminism remains essential: a language of women’s liberation can serve domination when it reduces an entire society to its treatment of women and assigns rescue to imperial power.

The book is broad, and its emphasis on elite texts and Egypt cannot represent the full diversity of Muslim women’s lives. Later scholarship has complicated its account through studies of race, labor, sexuality, non-Arab societies, and everyday religious practice. Those limits do not weaken its central intervention. They show why a historical genealogy must remain open to more locations and archives.

For feminist readers, the book replaces the sterile choice between defending religion and denouncing it. It asks who interprets, which institutions authorize an interpretation, and how colonial and national projects make gender into a political symbol. That shift makes women historical actors in debates over Islam rather than evidence used by others in a civilizational argument.
