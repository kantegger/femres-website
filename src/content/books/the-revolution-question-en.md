Julie D. Shayne begins with a deceptively simple double question: what do women do for revolutions, and what do revolutions do for women? Through El Salvador, Chile, and Cuba, she follows women in armed struggle, clandestine organizing, neighborhood work, exile, electoral politics, and postwar activism. The comparison prevents “revolutionary woman” from becoming a single heroic type and shows how different political trajectories shape the possibilities for feminism.

Drawing heavily on interviews with activists, Shayne documents labor that revolutionary histories often classify as secondary even when movements depend on it. Women transported messages and weapons, maintained households under repression, organized health and education, recruited communities, and developed political analysis. Yet leadership cultures frequently preserved masculine authority, treating gender equality as a concern to be postponed until after victory or democracy.

The book’s key argument is that revolutionary participation can generate feminist consciousness even when revolutionary institutions resist feminism. The contradiction between women’s sacrifice and their later marginalization becomes politically productive: veterans and organizers acquire networks, confidence, and a language for naming inequality. In Chile, dictatorship and democratization reshape feminist alliances; in El Salvador, war and peace produce both openings and disillusionment; in Cuba, state-led equality creates gains while constraining autonomous organization.

Shayne’s comparative method is especially useful because it refuses the easy claim that left politics automatically delivers gender justice. Class struggle, anti-imperialism, and national liberation can expand women’s political agency, but they do not dissolve domestic labor, sexual hierarchy, or organizational sexism. Feminism emerges both inside revolutionary projects and against their limits.

Published in 2004, the study cannot address later feminist strikes, digital organizing, or the region’s contemporary anti-rights backlash. Its interviews also reflect the movements and generations available to the researcher. Even so, it remains a sharp guide for reading any emancipatory project: measure it not only by its declared future, but by whose labor it uses, whose demands it delays, and who is permitted to define the revolution after the fighting ends.
