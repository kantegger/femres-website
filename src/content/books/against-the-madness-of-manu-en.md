*Against the Madness of Manu* is both a selection of B. R. Ambedkar’s writings, introduced and arranged by sociologist Sharmila Rege, and a deliberate intervention in intellectual history. Rege argues that Indian sociology and mainstream feminism have often confined Ambedkar to constitutional law, minority rights, or the category of “Dalit leader,” missing his early account of how caste depends on gendered order. The three sections—caste as endogamy, the madness of Manu, and the “unwept and unsung” death—reassemble texts from different moments so that anti-caste thought appears inside feminist theory rather than beside it as a special-interest concern.

The first line of argument begins with Ambedkar’s 1916 paper “Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis and Development.” He identifies endogamy as the mechanism by which a caste closes and reproduces itself. If a group must ensure that members marry only within it, it must manage the supposed “surplus” of women and men in its marriage pool. Enforced widowhood, prohibitions on remarriage, child marriage, and control over women’s sexuality and choice therefore cease to be unrelated backward customs; they become technologies for maintaining caste boundaries. The family moves from a private sphere to the political center, and a feminism that seeks equality while leaving endogamy intact risks preserving caste’s engine.

“Manu” invokes the authority associated with the *Manusmriti*, but Ambedkar does not simply blame one ancient lawgiver for inventing caste. He studies how scripture, priestly interpretation, social custom, and state law authorize one another until graded hierarchy appears sacred and natural. The burning of the *Manusmriti* during the Mahad struggle in 1927 was therefore a rejection both of a text and of its claim to final jurisdiction over dignity and social relations. By placing symbolic revolt alongside institutional analysis, Rege prevents the critique of Brahmanism from becoming a vague appeal for modern attitudes.

This produces the collection’s central feminist insight: Brahmanical patriarchy is not a universal patriarchy suffered identically by all women. Dominant-caste women’s sexuality and marriage are tightly regulated to secure lineage, property, and ritual purity; Dalit and laboring-caste women face economic extraction together with sexual violence, public humiliation, and exclusion from the protections attached to “respectable” femininity. Women in different locations can be subordinated by patriarchy while holding unequal interests and powers within caste. Any account of “women’s experience” that leaves caste location unnamed can mistake the life of the most privileged for the whole.

The selections carry this sociology into law and the state. The Hindu Code Bill associated with Ambedkar sought changes to marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption, and women’s property rights; after its repeated obstruction, he resigned as law minister in 1951. For Rege, his feminist significance lies not in a handful of enlightened statements but in treating kinship and property as democratic infrastructure. Equal citizenship in public is incomplete when birth hierarchy and male authority still distribute resources inside the household. Legal reform is necessary, though never sufficient without changes in social power.

The editorial method is itself part of the argument. Rege’s introductions reconstruct each controversy and trace how Ambedkar’s insights survived in Maharashtra through Dalit song troupes, movement booklets, and public memory even when academic sociology and women’s studies neglected them. She does not merely translate an archive for an uninformed mainstream; she asks who has the authority to call something theory while relegating community knowledge to memory or performance. The anthology thus revises both a feminist genealogy and the hierarchy of knowledge production.

Readers should not turn the collection into a flawless icon of Ambedkar. Its editorial architecture establishes a compelling lineage but cannot substitute for his full corpus, shifting political strategies, and historical contradictions. A framework centered on Hindu law does not automatically encompass the distinct legal and lived realities of Muslim, Christian, Adivasi, queer, and trans people, and some period language must be historicized rather than adopted. To inherit Rege’s method is to let Dalit feminists continue criticizing and extending Ambedkar, not to end inquiry through reverence.

Follow the sequence endogamy, scriptural authority, graded violence, and legal reconstruction, asking at every stage whose boundary a rule protects. Pair the book with Uma Chakravarti’s work on gendering caste for historical mechanism, Dalit women’s testimonies and *The Prisons We Broke* for everyday life, and *Annihilation of Caste* for the deeper conflict between sacred authority and democracy. Its enduring value to FemRes is the demonstration that caste is not a variable feminism can add later: governing women’s bodies, marriages, labor, and inheritance is among the conditions that make caste possible.
