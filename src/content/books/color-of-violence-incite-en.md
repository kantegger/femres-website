*Color of Violence* emerged from the organizing of INCITE! Women of Color Against Violence. It asks not how to make an existing anti-violence movement more diverse, but how “violence” itself changes when women of color, trans people, and gender-nonconforming people are placed at the center of analysis. Its thirty pieces include theory, position papers, letters, poetry, personal reflection, and organizing experience. They refuse to isolate domestic abuse and sexual assault from other forms of power, and refuse to make women of color visible only when a mainstream feminist institution needs a survivor’s story.

The anthology expands violence to include colonial occupation, militarism, border enforcement, policing, imprisonment, reproductive control, economic deprivation, medicine, and adoption policy. Sexual violence on the U.S.–Mexico border, Indigenous sovereignty, Palestine, immigration raids, disability oppression, and state violence against Black women appear on one map. The claim is not that every harm is identical. It is that domination inside a household draws on conditions created by states, capital, and colonial rule. Whether a person can leave abuse depends on housing, status, income, language, healthcare, and freedom from police violence—not only on willingness to seek help.

This makes INCITE!’s criticism of mainstream anti-violence institutions especially sharp. Shelters, mandatory arrest, prosecution, and sentence enhancements helped win public recognition of gender violence, yet they can hand survivors to a racialized criminal system. Black women may be arrested for self-defense; migrants may expose their status by calling police; jurisdictional conflict affecting Indigenous women cannot be solved by more local policing; trans people and sex workers are often treated as risks before they are treated as victims. State protection has never been evenly distributed. Treating police as feminism’s natural ally makes the most heavily policed communities pay for someone else’s safety.

The joint statement by INCITE! and Critical Resistance on gender violence and the prison-industrial complex is a foundational abolition feminist text. It refuses two equally dangerous shortcuts: opposition to policing cannot minimize rape and abuse within communities, and protection of survivors cannot justify expanding a system organized through race, poverty, and gender norms. Community accountability is not a gentle compromise. It is the difficult double work of developing the capacity to stop harm, support survivors, and constrain responsible people while resisting police, prison, and banishment as the only available response.

The collection’s global and cross-issue design also changes the meaning of coalition. “Women of color” is not an internally uniform identity; Black, Indigenous, Arab, Asian, Latina, migrant, disabled, and trans organizers carry distinct histories and interests. Solidarity cannot conceal these differences through a claim of shared oppression. It must analyze how white supremacy, settler colonialism, capitalism, and heteropatriarchy organize violence differently. The placement of poems, testimony, and movement documents beside scholarship also insists that organizing knowledge need not first be translated into university language to count as theory.

The book’s major practical contribution is to extend anti-violence work beyond services delivered after a crisis into movement building. It addresses collective leadership, cross-community alliances, harm within shelter systems, grassroots accountability, and cultural production. Organizations are asked to examine how they reproduce control: who can enter a service, who counts as a “good victim,” and who is excluded for anger, poverty, drug use, disability, or refusal to call police. Safety means more than removing one person; it means giving people the resources, relationships, and political power not to be forced back into danger.

Most material in *Color of Violence* originated in the early twenty-first century. A 2016 reissue does not mean every law, term, or strategy has been updated. The thirty contributors do not hold identical positions, and the volume’s global reach sometimes makes a place an entry point rather than a sustained account. Community accountability can be appropriated by powerful people, while an anti-state position cannot excuse an organization from providing immediate protection. Later work on Black trans life, disability justice, Indigenous law, digital surveillance, and survivor-centered practice is needed to test how the framework has developed.

For FemRes, *Color of Violence* offers a way to redraw causality. Whenever a policy promises to “protect women,” ask which violence it names as urgent and which state or economic violence it renders invisible; who can safely use the institution and who may lose children, status, housing, or freedom; and whether affected communities designed the response. The answer is not withdrawal of public responsibility. It is an anti-violence politics accountable at once for intimate harm, colonial power, racialized punishment, and material deprivation.
