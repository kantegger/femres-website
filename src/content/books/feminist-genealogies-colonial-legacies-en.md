Feminist Genealogies, Colonial Legacies, Democratic Futures proposed in the 1990s a transnational feminism that remains demanding: do not derive a universal women's question from Euro-American experience; build comparison from relations among capitalism, colonial rule, nation-states, and situated resistance. Editors M. Jacqui Alexander and Chandra Talpade Mohanty use genealogy to trace the historical making of power and struggle, not a family tree in which feminism diffuses outward from a Western origin.

The first section connects labor, nationalism, state practice, and colonial inheritance. Women workers are organized within a common global system while divided by race, nationality, and wage hierarchy. Postcolonial states may subordinate gender and sexual politics to national unity; advanced capitalist states reproduce colonial governance through migration and policing. “Third World” and “West” are therefore not sealed geographies but unequal positions co-produced within an interdependent political economy.

Alexander's analysis of erotic autonomy in the Bahamian tourist economy demonstrates that sexuality is not a cultural side issue. States can make heterosexual family, tourist consumption, and serviceable women's bodies part of development while punishing desires outside the norm. Indigenous women's struggles over civil rights and sovereignty likewise show that individual equality may not protect land or collective political existence. Democracy that recognizes only abstract citizens can misname assimilation as liberation.

The second part approaches identity, culture, and knowledge through Chicana feminism, Black female sexuality, prison testimonios, and transnational cinema. Identity is neither natural nor pure. Silence itself has an institutional history: archives, medicine, family, and media decide which sexual lives can be spoken and which subjects appear. Identity politics matters not because it fixes group boundaries but because it exposes their manufacture by states, capital, and culture, creating grounds for coalition.

The final section turns to Sistren in Jamaica, the Hyderabad women's movement, Women in Nigeria, South Asian immigrant antiviolence work, and Afro-Surinamese organizing. “Democratic futures” reside in practice rather than an abstract electoral endpoint. Collective cultural production, internal debate, violence work, and mutual accountability are sites where democracy is learned. Theory must answer to organizing experience, while organizations must analyze their own classed, racial, and sexual power.

The anthology's period language needs historicization. Even when used relationally, the West/Third World framework may not capture trans life, disability, climate displacement, or digital capitalism; the politics surrounding each case has also changed. Chapters vary in theoretical density, and geographic breadth cannot replace continued study of particular movements. Later Black, Indigenous, queer, and abolitionist transnational scholarship should accompany it.

Its lasting achievement is to distinguish solidarity from liberal multicultural inclusion. Transnational comparison must follow how capital, state violence, and colonial history connect locations while respecting the concepts and aims of local organizers. For FemRes, the book offers a feminist measure of democracy: whether institutions redistribute control over work, land, bodies, and knowledge, and whether marginalized groups can organize and define their futures rather than merely enter an existing order.
