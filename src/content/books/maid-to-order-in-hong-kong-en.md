Nicole Constable’s ethnography studies migrant domestic work not as an abstract flow of care but as a lived institution in Hong Kong. The 2007 second edition revisits an earlier study in light of the 1997 handover, the Asian financial crisis, and the SARS outbreak, while adding the growing presence of Indonesian workers to its original focus on Filipinas. These shifts show that a household employment relation is continuously remade by regional history.

The book’s method combines participant observation, interviews, public discourse, and workers’ accounts. It opens with domestic workers gathering in Central on Sundays, transforming a financial district into a social, religious, commercial, and political space on their day off. That scene matters analytically: workers who are isolated inside separate homes become a visible collective, producing information, friendship, worship, pleasure, and strategies for survival.

Constable then connects local demand to global inequality. As more middle-class Hong Kong women entered paid employment and local domestic labor declined, employers recruited women from the Philippines and later Indonesia. Economic crises in sending countries, government reliance on remittances, and recruitment agencies helped create the supply. The resulting job is neither a spontaneous cultural fit nor a private arrangement; states and markets actively assemble it.

The chapter on “superior servants” exposes the racial and colonial memories inside employer preference. Nostalgia for an allegedly better Cantonese servant becomes a standard against which foreign workers are judged, while national stereotypes rank Filipina and Indonesian women by language, education, obedience, or supposed character. These comparisons turn political and economic differences into personal traits, allowing unequal treatment to appear like rational consumer choice.

Recruitment and training extend this discipline before a worker enters the employer’s home. Agencies, governments, and employers all benefit when workers are prepared to be compliant, efficient, and remittance-producing. Inside the household, rules can reach beyond completed tasks to the worker’s body, voice, emotions, food, rest, and privacy. Temporary migrant status limits the practical value of a contract when reporting abuse may also threaten income, residence, or future employment.

Yet the ethnography does not mistake discipline for total submission. Its later chapters distinguish organized protest, everyday resistance, self-discipline, and pleasure, showing that agency can take forms other than dramatic rebellion. Negotiating a rule, sharing knowledge, occupying public space on a Sunday, maintaining religious and friendship networks, or protecting a private self can all interrupt an employer’s claim to complete control without making structural constraints disappear.

The second edition remains anchored in fieldwork from the 1990s and early 2000s. Hong Kong’s worker population, legal rules, wage levels, communication technologies, and organizing landscape have changed since then, and an ethnographer’s account cannot represent every Filipina or Indonesian worker. The book also centers women in a sector whose gendering should itself be questioned. Current worker-led sources are necessary to establish what persists and what has shifted.

Read *Maid to Order in Hong Kong* by keeping its public and private scenes together. Compare Sunday in Central with the household rules, then trace how a recruitment promise changes as it passes through an agency, visa, standard contract, employer, and worker network. Pair each institutional claim with a current account from Hong Kong domestic-worker organizations. The result is a feminist map of the city in which the home, border, market, and public square are parts of one labor regime.
