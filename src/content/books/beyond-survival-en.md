*Beyond Survival* begins from an immediate dilemma: if calling the police may expose a Black person, migrant, trans person, someone in psychiatric crisis, or a family member to further harm, and you do not know what else to do, what becomes possible? Edited by Ejeris Dixon and Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha, the collection does not turn “do not call 911” into a moral command. It gathers tools, failures, relationships, and organizational knowledge from transformative-justice practice. The question most often directed at abolition—what will you do without police and prisons?—is moved from the debate stage back into the crisis itself.

Transformative justice here is neither mediation nor a demand that a person apologize. It pursues at least four aims at once: safety and self-determination for the person harmed; concrete accountability and behavioral change for the person responsible; repair of relationships and collective capacities affected by the harm; and transformation of the material and institutional conditions that made violence more likely. Unlike a narrow idea of “restoring” the past, transformation recognizes that the prior family, organization, or community may already have been unjust. The goal is not to return everyone to before, but to build relations that neither depend on state punishment nor reproduce its logic of disposability.

The collection’s value lies in the specificity of its methods. Essays and interviews address responding to emotional or psychiatric crisis without immediately summoning police, creating safer parties and community safety zones, helping survivors establish boundaries, accompanying someone who caused harm through accountability, and organizing under the pressure of death, abuse, or immigration enforcement. Groups such as Creative Interventions, Trans Lifeline, the Audre Lorde Project, and the Bay Area Transformative Justice Collective do not supply one universal procedure. They show the labor of risk assessment, support-team formation, clear task assignment, documented commitments, and long follow-up.

The tension between survivor-centered practice and the belief that people who cause harm can change runs throughout the book. Transformative justice refuses to define a person permanently as a disposable monster, because that classification readily repeats prison logic. It also refuses to make the survivor educate, forgive, or remain in contact with that person. Accountability may require distance, loss of a position, return of resources, sustained supervision, and a reorganization of daily life. Outcomes cannot be determined only by the most prestigious members of an organization, nor can surface unity be preserved by sacrificing the choices of the person harmed.

Disability justice and the experience of queer communities of color make the book’s account of “community capacity” unusually material. Responding without police requires accessible healthcare, interpretation, transport, food, housing, rest, and networks that know the people involved. Good intentions cannot produce these automatically. Many behaviors labeled uncooperative or dangerous are entangled with trauma, illness, poverty, racialized policing, and the absence of care. Incorporating those conditions into safety planning makes justice more than a meeting held after an incident; it becomes an infrastructure of survival built beforehand.

The collection also corrects a romantic idea of transformative justice: communities are not naturally safe. Friends protect friends, organizations suppress complaints to preserve reputation, and charismatic leaders manipulate the language of complexity to evade responsibility. Effective intervention depends on trust, but also needs explicit boundaries, outside support, conflict skills, and enough time. If the state withdraws without transferring resources, accountability and care become a second unpaid job for a small number of Black, Indigenous, queer, disabled women and nonbinary organizers, eventually ending in exhaustion.

The book therefore does not instruct every survivor to renounce police, protection orders, or legal options. In situations of continuing lethal violence, weapons, severe economic control, or risk to children, immediate separation and professional intervention may be indispensable. Welfare, healthcare, and legal conditions also differ sharply by place. Some cases in the collection presuppose years of relationships and a high level of organization; copying a tool list without those conditions may create new pressure. The challenge is not merely whether transformative justice can “scale,” but who has the resources to sustain an accountability process that may last for years.

For FemRes readers, *Beyond Survival* is best treated as an archive of practice rather than a purity test. For each case, ask what the survivor actually wants, who provides immediate safety, which verifiable changes the person responsible must make, which housing, healthcare, or income conditions must change, and how the team prevents burnout. Used alongside local hotlines, legal aid, disability support, and social-work resources, the book’s central promise becomes more credible: transformative justice is not faith that communities will always get it right, but the work of giving them the capacity not to surrender every crisis to violent institutions.
