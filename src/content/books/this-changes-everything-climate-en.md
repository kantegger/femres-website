Naomi Klein’s *This Changes Everything* argues that climate breakdown is not one policy problem among many but a civilizational challenge to deregulated capitalism. Decades of delay are not explained by a lack of technical solutions: renewable energy, public transit, conservation, and lower-carbon ways of organizing life are already imaginable. The obstacle is a political economy committed to endless growth, privatization, corporate power, and increasingly destructive extraction. Klein connects denial, weak market-based environmentalism, disaster capitalism, and geoengineering fantasies to the same refusal to limit accumulation or democratize control over land and energy.

The book’s alternative emerges through movements—especially Indigenous resistance and place-based struggles Klein describes as “Blockadia”—that connect emissions to sovereignty, labor, public services, and historical responsibility. This is where its feminist value becomes clearest, even though gender is not its central analytic category. Extractivism depends on devalued reproductive labor, sacrifice zones, colonial claims to land, and an ideal of mastery that ecofeminists have long challenged. Climate action can therefore expand public care and democratic participation, or it can reproduce dispossession through green markets and technocratic planning. The issue is not simply which energy source wins, but who controls transition and whose lives are treated as expendable.

Klein’s scale gives the book mobilizing power but sometimes compresses different movements and regions into a single anti-capitalist narrative. Readers should pair it with work led by Indigenous, Global South, Black, feminist, and disability-justice organizers who specify how climate harm and transition are unevenly lived. Still, the book decisively changed public climate language by shifting attention from individual consumption to institutions and power. For FemRes, it offers a bridge from climate concern to systemic politics: if the crisis changes everything, then a just response must transform ownership, care, work, and democracy rather than preserve the existing order with cleaner machinery.
