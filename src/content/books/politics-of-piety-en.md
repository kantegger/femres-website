Based on fieldwork in Cairo’s women’s mosque movement, Politics of Piety studies participants who teach scripture, cultivate religious virtues, and reshape public religious life. Saba Mahmood’s central provocation is that feminist analysis often recognizes agency only when a subject resists, subverts, or escapes a norm. The women she encounters do not fit that model: they deliberately inhabit disciplines of modesty, prayer, and moral self-formation.

Mahmood does not ask readers to celebrate submission. She asks them to distinguish understanding from endorsement. In the Aristotelian ethical tradition she draws upon, repeated bodily practices do not merely express an inner self; they help create capacities, desires, and forms of perception. A gesture of modesty can therefore be more than an external rule imposed on an otherwise free individual. It may belong to a cultivated way of becoming a particular kind of subject.

This argument unsettles secular-liberal assumptions about freedom. If freedom is defined in advance as autonomy from tradition, religious women can appear only as dupes or incomplete feminists. Mahmood shows that power also produces the languages through which people seek flourishing. The analytic task is to examine those formations without converting difference into either false consciousness or cultural authenticity.

The book has rightly attracted criticism. Its close attention to ethical practice can leave economic inequality, state repression, family coercion, and the consequences for those who reject religious norms less visible. The question of agency must not erase unequal power or the material cost of nonconformity. These debates are part of the book’s importance rather than a reason to avoid it.

Politics of Piety remains a demanding companion for feminist work across religious and secular contexts. It asks whether our concepts can encounter lives that do not mirror our preferred politics. At the same time, it invites a second question: how can careful understanding coexist with judgment about coercion and injustice? Holding both questions together is the book’s most durable challenge.
