*A Forgotten Liberator* does not simply install Savitribai Phule as a solitary heroine to be commemorated. Through biographical essays, three letters to Jotiba Phule, poems, photographs, a chronology, and student writing, it reconstructs the intellectual world of nineteenth-century anti-caste struggle in western India. “Forgotten” names a politics of historical selection: how could a thinker who joined women’s education to Shudra and Dalit emancipation, widows’ survival, and agrarian justice remain marginal to both upper-caste nationalist history and feminist histories centered on Europe and North America?

Savitribai and Jotiba opened a school for girls in Pune in 1848, but the collection shows that “first woman teacher” was never an unconflicted honorific. Teaching girls—especially girls from oppressed castes—threatened Brahman control of knowledge, patriarchal households, and the order reproduced through child marriage. The abuse and filth thrown at Savitribai on her way to teach have become emblematic, yet the deeper point is her conception of education: not individual advancement into an unchanged hierarchy, but a means for oppressed people to identify structures, organize with one another, and refuse the destiny assigned to them.

One of the volume’s most powerful inclusions is Mukta Salve’s indictment of the conditions imposed on Mang and Mahar communities, written while she was a student at a Phule school. Placing a Dalit girl’s analysis beside the reformers’ lives overturns the story of great benefactors giving education to passive recipients. The school produced people able to analyze religious authority, exploited labor, and organized humiliation. Savitribai’s poetry similarly casts learning as awakening and revolt, establishing her not as an assistant carrying out her husband’s program but as an author developing an anti-caste ethics of education in her own voice.

Her letters show how political commitments operated through kinship and everyday decisions. She defended an inter-caste couple against honor violence and worked with Jotiba to create support for widows, unmarried mothers, and infants at risk of being killed. These practices reveal caste as more than a ranked social taxonomy: it reproduces itself through the control of women’s marriage, sexuality, reproduction, and inheritance. Anti-caste politics must therefore be feminist, while women’s education that leaves marital discipline, sexual stigma, and material survival intact can merely admit a few people into the old order.

Savitribai later worked through the Satyashodhak Samaj, continued organizing after Jotiba’s death, and cared for people during the plague of 1897, when she contracted the illness and died. The collection locates these acts within collective struggles of peasants, workers, and oppressed castes. It resists a familiar domestication: praising her devotion to “education” while erasing her confrontation with Brahmanism, scriptural authority, and inherited hierarchy. Her pedagogy was not benevolence; it redistributed the power to interpret the world and to reject obedience.

The book also requires a critical reading. It is brief and explicitly reparative, and some biographical details come through later remembrance. Its focus on the Phule couple can leave the labor of collaborators such as Sagunabai, students, and wider non-Brahman networks less visible. Recovering one exceptional woman should not be the endpoint. The anthology’s own archival work invites further questions about who preserves evidence, how individual prestige is assigned, and why collective movement knowledge is repeatedly condensed into a small number of names.

For FemRes, the book rearranges the chronology and geography of feminist history. Girls’ schools, anti-caste verse, and support for widows in nineteenth-century Maharashtra were not local echoes of a theory imported later from the West; they were intellectual and institutional experiments generated by specific structures of oppression. Savitribai’s questions remain urgent: does education train people to function within hierarchy, or give them a language for dismantling it? Who enters the classroom, and whose experience is allowed to become knowledge?
