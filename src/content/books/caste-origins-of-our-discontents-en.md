*Caste: The Origins of Our Discontents* attempts to change the language through which Americans describe racial inequality. Isabel Wilkerson argues that “racism” is often heard as a matter of individual prejudice, malice, or backward belief, allowing people to use their own good intentions as evidence that the system has improved. “Caste” directs attention instead to a hierarchy that survives personal intent. Skin color is the principal marker assigned to it in the United States, but the order is sustained by rules about birth, purity, occupation, marriage, dignity, and obedience. The new term does not make race irrelevant. It asks how the underlying structure that determines who is believed, protected, employed, or allowed near power can persist after openly racist language becomes unacceptable.

Wilkerson compares the anti-Black hierarchy of the United States, the Indian caste order, and Nazi Germany, identifying eight “pillars”: divine or natural justification, heritability, endogamy, boundaries of purity and pollution, occupational hierarchy, dehumanization and stigma, terror as enforcement, and belief in the inherent superiority or inferiority of groups. The point of comparison is not that the three histories are identical. It is to identify a recurring engineering of rank: a contingent power relation is first narrated as an immutable difference, then law, custom, and everyday punishment train people to inhabit the positions assigned to them.

The book moves between macrohistory, reportage, personal stories, and Wilkerson’s own experiences. She writes about residential segregation, labor, medical abuse, and humiliation in public space, but also about the small interactions that determine who speaks first in a room, who is served, and who must continually prove qualification. This narrative method makes “structure” tangible. Hierarchy lives not only in a Supreme Court decision or a demographic table, but in bodily vigilance, narrowed expectations, and the ease with which a dominant group need not recognize its own position. Abolishing discriminatory law is therefore indispensable, yet insufficient to erase social reflexes accumulated over centuries.

For feminist readers, the analysis of endogamy, lineage, and purity is especially important. Any hierarchy that depends on inheritance must regulate intimacy, reproduction, and women’s bodies: it must determine who may marry whom, whose sexuality is treated as available for appropriation, and who is made responsible for maintaining the group boundary. The reproductive exploitation of Black women under U.S. slavery and the maintenance of caste through marriage and sexual violence in India cannot be exchanged for one another. They do, however, demonstrate that gender is not an extra axis added to race or caste. As Black feminist and Dalit feminist thinkers have long argued, hierarchy is reproduced where family, labor, and state violence meet.

The book’s broadest contribution is to shift attention from “who is a bad person?” to “what structure rewards domination?” It explains how members of a dominant group may inherit protection through housing, professional networks, schools, and presumptions of credibility without expressing explicit hatred, while those below bear suspicion, surveillance, and punishment for crossing an assigned boundary. Wilkerson therefore asks for more than kinder interpersonal conduct. Institutions that repeatedly manufacture unequal outcomes must be dismantled even when the individuals operating them describe themselves as fair.

Using caste as a transhistorical framework has nevertheless generated serious criticism. Wilkerson sometimes treats India as a mirror for explaining the United States without giving sufficient theoretical centrality to B. R. Ambedkar, Dalit thinkers, and contemporary anti-caste movements. The phrase “American caste” can also compress the distinctive histories of slavery, capitalism, colonialism, and state formation into a static ladder. Conversely, caste in India is not sustained by social psychology and ritual alone; it is entangled with land, labor, religious authority, and party politics. A comparison that tracks only resonant images of humiliation can obscure how different systems must be changed in materially different ways.

The book’s gender analysis also requires feminist extension. Wilkerson illuminates endogamy and sexual violence, but does not fully develop theories of Brahmanical patriarchy or Black feminist accounts of capital, care, and state violence. Her recurring appeal to empathy and moral awakening can return a structural diagnosis to the register of personal attitude. *Caste* should therefore be read with Ambedkar, Dalit women’s writing, the Black historical tradition in which Wilkerson herself works, and scholarship by thinkers such as Angela Davis and Patricia Hill Collins. This does not invalidate comparison; it makes comparison answerable to the intellectual traditions being compared.

For FemRes, *Caste* is best used as an entry point into the naturalization of hierarchy, not as a final explanation of either Indian caste or anti-Black oppression in the United States. It teaches readers to ask how a classification becomes identity, how identity is fixed as destiny, how intimacy and labor police a boundary, and why reform so often preserves a structure after changing its name. Holding on to those questions while refusing to turn distinct histories into an elegant equivalence is the book’s most durable contribution to intersectional feminist method.
