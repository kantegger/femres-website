*The Doctor and the Saint* began as Arundhati Roy’s book-length introduction to a 2014 annotated edition of *Annihilation of Caste* and was later issued independently. The “doctor” is B. R. Ambedkar, diagnosing caste as a social disease whose religious and material supports must be severed; the “saint” is M. K. Gandhi, canonized by the Indian state and global traditions of nonviolence as a moral figure. Roy does not offer balanced parallel biographies. She asks why Gandhi’s near-divine status dominates national memory while Ambedkar’s foundational challenge to hierarchy is often reduced to constitutional technique or a sectional Dalit demand.

The central confrontation reaches back to 1936. Lahore reform group Jat-Pat Todak Mandal invited Ambedkar to address its conference but canceled it after he refused to remove his attack on Hindu scriptures and priestly authority; he then published *Annihilation of Caste* himself. Ambedkar argued that shared meals and benevolent reform could not destroy caste because caste was not an accidental prejudice but graded inequality authorized as sacred. Unless scriptural infallibility was relinquished, custom would restore the boundary. Gandhi answered in *Harijan*, opposing untouchability but trying to distinguish it from a purified varna order. Roy makes visible the distance between reforming a tradition and rejecting the foundation of rank.

The 1932 Poona Pact had already turned this philosophical difference into a struggle over representation. Britain’s Communal Award proposed separate electorates for the “Depressed Classes,” enabling them to choose representatives without dependence on dominant-caste votes. Gandhi opposed it with a prison fast, arguing that it would divide Hindu society. Under enormous moral pressure, Ambedkar accepted reserved seats within joint electorates instead. The number of reserved seats grew, but Dalit candidates still faced an electorate dominated by caste Hindus. For Roy, who may speak for themselves is not procedural detail; it reveals who is authorized to define national unity.

To dismantle the saintly image, Roy revisits Gandhi’s remarks about Black Africans during his South African years, his long relationship to hereditary occupation and varna, and his ties to wealthy industrialists. Moral prestige derived from nonviolence and personal simplicity does not automatically equal a commitment to structural equality. A leader may oppose the cruelties of untouchability while placing occupational order and social harmony above independent organization by subordinated people. This archive prevents a late symbol from erasing earlier positions, although Gandhi’s views did change, and any single line drawn across decades of political life invites legitimate historical dispute.

Roy brings the debate into the present rather than leaving it as a quarrel between great men. Manual scavenging, land dispossession, caste atrocity, and the caste- and gendered violence inflicted on Surekha Bhotmange’s family at Khairlanji in 2006 show that constitutional equality did not dissolve social order. She also asks how liberalization and corporate power coexist with old hierarchy: a nation praised internationally for democracy and growth can continue assigning its dirtiest and most dangerous labor through inherited status. Independence from colonial rule and freedom from caste rule are not synonyms.

Feminist readers can trace purity, endogamy, land, and sexual violence as a single caste infrastructure, while also recognizing work the essay does not do. Dalit women’s bodies often appear as sites proving caste’s brutality, but their organizing, theory, and ordinary labor do not organize Roy’s narrative; Ambedkar’s work on women’s rights and kinship law is not central either. Mentioning gendered atrocity does not make the book a complete Dalit feminist account. *Against the Madness of Manu*, Dalit women’s testimony, and movement histories must hold interpretive authority over that missing field.

The publication itself generated a political dispute that belongs inside the review. Some Dalit writers and activists objected to making a globally celebrated non-Dalit author’s enormous introduction the gateway to *Annihilation of Caste*, arguing that marketing once again let a privileged voice explain, frame, and potentially eclipse Ambedkar. Other readers credit Roy with carrying an ignored debate to a broad international public. Critics also charge that she organizes Ambedkar too completely around Gandhi and selects historical evidence polemically. Studying this controversy enacts the book’s own question: who controls introduction, framing, and the cultural capital produced from Dalit thought?

The responsible sequence is to treat *The Doctor and the Saint* as a positioned polemic, then read *Annihilation of Caste* and the original Ambedkar–Gandhi exchanges directly, alongside Dalit critiques of Roy and the publishing project. Pair it with *Against the Madness of Manu* on gender and kinship and *Coming Out as Dalit* on contemporary elite spaces. Its value to FemRes is not a verdict naming an absolute hero, but a discipline of deconsecrated public reading: every national-liberation story must answer to the people still subordinated by its version of unity.
