Female Husbands studies a historical term that once circulated widely and later disappeared from public language. Jen Manion follows people assigned female at birth who lived, worked, and married women as men in Britain from roughly 1740 to 1840 and the United States from about 1830 to 1910. Rather than treat “female husband” as a stable self-identity, Manion understands it as a category newspapers, courts, and publics used to explain these marriages, opening a record of lives more complex than the gender terms that later replaced it.

These people most often entered archives when their histories were exposed: an examination after death, a request for poor relief, an arrest, marital conflict, or a newspaper spectacle determined whose life survived on paper. The sources are consequently precious and hostile. Reports compress decades into a story of a woman deceiving another woman, legal records fixate on bodily inspection, and archives preserve the language of exposers more readily than a subject's own account. Manion shows that history does not transparently reveal the past; police, physicians, and editors mediated the gender-variant lives we can now read.

“Husband” was a social position, not merely a change of clothes. Living as a man could offer higher wages, occupational mobility, freedom to travel, greater safety in public, and the possibility of building a household with a woman. Cases such as James and Mary Howe demonstrate how marriage, labor, and masculinity sustained one another: a person acquired social recognition by providing for a household, running a business, and performing a husband's duties. Class is decisive because workers, itinerant laborers, and poor people faced greater exposure through relief, housing, and criminal systems while leaving fewer private letters that might explain their choices.

The book also releases wives from the formula of the “deceived woman.” Many likely knew their partner's bodily history and participated in building a marriage, livelihood, and household. Reporters and officials had reason to erase that knowledge, because acknowledging a woman's deliberate choice of such a relationship unsettled the claim that heterosexual marriage naturally joined two fixed sexes. A wife's silence cannot simply prove ignorance; it may record an attempt to protect a partner, an income, and a shared life. Marriage appears here as collaborative practice rather than an institution guaranteed by anatomy.

Manion explains why the category of female husband eventually vanished. As medicine, law, and sexology increasingly made sex assigned at birth the final truth of a person, sustained social roles lost authority to bodily classification; the press shifted from describing a husband to diagnosing a “woman.” Gender diversity did not first emerge with modern medicine. Modern institutions instead narrowed the language in which such lives could be understood. The binary appears not as timeless common sense but as a historical product repeatedly reinforced by archives, professional expertise, and state power.

One of the book's most careful decisions is not to assign every person a single retrospective identity. We cannot know who would choose the contemporary words transgender, nonbinary, lesbian, or something else. But avoiding anachronism cannot justify returning everyone to the label of women disguised as men. Manion holds uncertainty alongside the evidence of sustained male names, pronouns, occupations, and marital positions. The method offers an ethics for trans history: do not seize the dead's power of self-naming, but do not surrender that power entirely to a hostile archive either.

The book principally covers the Anglo-American world, and surviving evidence overrepresents white subjects and working-class people caught by legal systems; it cannot stand for every gender-variant life, while racial and colonial formations require further histories. Even so, Female Husbands makes a major feminist and trans intervention by returning wages, housing, reputation, mobility, and wives' agency to the history of gender. Its purpose is not to find uncomplicated ancestors for present identities, but to show how institutions manufacture “intelligible” gender—and how people built shared lives in the spaces those institutions failed to close.
