*Fumbling Towards Repair* begins by telling readers not to mistake it for an introduction. Mariame Kaba and Shira Hassan wrote this workbook for people already involved in formal community-accountability processes: facilitators, mediators, survivor-support people, and those coordinating sustained follow-through. It is neither a read-and-certify training course nor a book of universal answers. “Fumbling” belongs in the title because responding to interpersonal harm requires experience, judgment, relationship, and revision rather than the clean application of a political slogan.

The authors' authority is practical. Kaba's organizing connects youth incarceration, racial justice, gendered violence, and prison-industrial-complex abolition; Hassan brings decades of harm-reduction work, organizing with young people in street economies, and community accountability. They derive questions from processes that went wrong, stopped, or had to begin again rather than deducing a procedure from abstract ethics. The workbook consequently turns its scrutiny toward the facilitator: What skills and relationships do you possess? Why do you want to intervene? What can you sustain, and which of your motives may displace the needs of the person harmed?

Community accountability here is not private reconciliation, and it does not require a survivor to forgive. It attempts several difficult things together: stop ongoing harm; center the survivor's safety and self-determination; require the person who caused harm to understand impact, make repair, and change behavior; and transform the conditions that allowed violence to continue. This connects it to transformative justice but cannot be reduced to one conversation or apology. Without observable behavioral change, resource redistribution, and plans against recurrence, “accountability” can remain a ritual for protecting a community's reputation.

As a workbook, it offers reflection questions, skill assessments, definitions, activities, hard-learned stories, sample documents, and troubleshooting prompts rather than one continuous argument. Exercises are designed for facilitators working alone and for situations involving survivors, people who caused harm, and support teams. They ask readers to map interests, confidentiality boundaries, information flows, realistic goals, and how commitments will be recorded and reviewed. The point is not to bureaucratize relationships but to leave a shared, testable process when pressure, loyalty, and fear distort memory.

The book pays particular attention to facilitation power. A well-intentioned coordinator can still take decisions from a survivor, put personal political purity above safety, or concentrate on “saving” the person who caused harm while risk continues. Deciding whether to enter a process, when to withdraw, how to co-facilitate, and how to address conflicts of interest or one's own trauma are themselves accountability questions. Repair also need not mean restoring a prior relationship; responsible outcomes may include lasting separation, leaving an organization, material restitution, or accepting that a relationship cannot be recovered.

Its importance to abolition feminism lies in refusing a forced choice between opposing violence and opposing incarceration. Police, courts, and prisons can expose Black, Indigenous, migrant, queer, trans, disabled, and poor survivors to renewed surveillance and punishment, but naming that fact does not make community inherently safe. Abolition requires alternative capacity: dependable crisis support, sustained accompaniment, material resources, durable constraints on people who cause harm, and a collective memory not organized by public shaming. Otherwise, removing state tools merely returns the burden to the most isolated person.

That is also the workbook's crucial boundary. The authors explicitly advise people without formal community-accountability experience to begin with more introductory, step-by-step resources. A group without trust, housing, health care, interpretation, or safe space cannot manufacture infrastructure from worksheets. Communities can reproduce racism, misogyny, ableism, and status hierarchies; in immediate danger, a survivor may autonomously choose medical, legal, or other outside support. When adapting a method rooted in U.S. movement contexts, “do not call the police” must never become a new command imposed on survivors.

The best use is therefore collective and slow. An experienced group can first build shared language with introductory tools such as the Creative Interventions toolkit, then select exercises suited to the current stage and record disagreements, stopping conditions, and a review date. At every point it should ask whether the survivor still has choices, whether change by the person who caused harm is verifiable, and whether supporters are depleted. Read beside local anti-violence, disability-justice, and harm-reduction resources, the workbook makes an honest promise: people may fumble and fail, but imperfection cannot justify outsourcing responsibility for harm back to punishment.
