African Feminism assembled case studies from ten countries at a moment when structural adjustment, democratization, conflict, and postcolonial state-building were reshaping everyday survival. Its contributors locate feminist politics in access to land, food distribution, wages, health care, property, markets, and elections. The volume’s well-known phrase—‘bread, butter, culture, and power’—rejects the idea that material survival is somehow less feminist than debates about identity or representation.

The cases also complicate the boundary between formal organizations and ordinary political labor. Market women, farmers, professionals, party activists, and community organizers negotiate with states, kinship institutions, religious authority, and development agencies. Their strategies may include confrontation, coalition, maternal claims, or the tactical use of culturally legible roles. Feminist agency therefore appears not as a single ideology but as work conducted inside unequal and changing institutions.

Mikell’s introduction distinguishes this politics from dominant Western feminist agendas and describes much African feminism of the period as heterosexual and pronatal. That historical claim is useful but no longer adequate as a definition. Queer African feminisms, struggles for sexual autonomy, and new movement networks have made visible constituencies that the anthology marginalizes. The book should be read as a map of a particular period, not a boundary around what African feminism may become.

Another tension concerns the scale of ‘Africa.’ The volume resists treating the continent as homogeneous, yet its comparative ambition can still encourage broad claims across different colonial histories, classes, religions, and political economies. Its strongest chapters work precisely because they stay close to institutions and conflicts. They show why local evidence must discipline transnational theory.

The collection remains valuable for connecting feminism to political economy. Representation in parliament cannot substitute for control over land or safe work; legal equality means little when austerity transfers public care into women’s unpaid labor. For FemRes, it offers both a foundation and a historical benchmark against which newer African feminist movements can be read.
