*From Where We Stand* treats antiwar women as producers of theory rather than cases waiting for an academic to explain them. Cynthia Cockburn works across Sierra Leone, India, Colombia, Serbia, and Israel/Palestine, as well as transnational networks opposing Western imperial wars. She begins with how participants understand their own locations and builds an analysis of war, gender, and organizing from that situated knowledge.

The book refuses the essentialist claim that men are warlike and women naturally peaceful. Cockburn instead studies how a violent gender order rewards militarized masculinity and connects it to class power, racialization, and national projects. Women can support nationalism and war; organizing “as women” is not biological destiny but a strategic use of shared, though unequal, gendered locations.

Cases of Serbian women refusing loyalty to a warring state and Palestinian and Israeli women refusing absolute enmity show both the necessity and danger of cross-conflict solidarity. Participants cannot erase asymmetry: occupier and occupied, citizen and dispossessed cannot be reconciled through “both sides” language. Solidarity means confronting difference and taking responsibility for violence committed in one’s own name.

Cockburn also follows networks opposing the “war on terror” and imperialism, then examines WILPF’s work with the United Nations. Institutional access can supply language, resources, and leverage, but can also translate radical demands into project metrics and subordinate grassroots labor to professional peacebuilding. The book keeps achievement and compromise in the same frame.

Its major contribution is to understand nationalism, racism, capitalism, patriarchy, and militarism as mutually reinforcing rather than rival explanations. War is not a sudden interruption of peace; it is prepared through budgets, borders, household divisions, weapons economies, and stories of enemies. Feminist antimilitarism opposes bombs and the everyday institutions that make bombs appear reasonable.

The method has limits. Cockburn openly identifies with women’s peace organizing, so representativeness and researcher position require scrutiny. The framework says relatively little about nonbinary, trans, and queer antiwar actors. Cross-regional comparison reveals structures but cannot replace the colonial histories and local knowledge particular to each conflict.

For FemRes, the book offers less a roster of admirable groups than a method for testing peace politics. Does a movement confront inequality within its own ranks? Does national unity silence feminist criticism? Can it oppose imperial power without excusing local patriarchy? “Where we stand” matters because every antiwar claim is made from a specific position within power.
