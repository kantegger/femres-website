A Feminist Voyage through International Relations collects major essays from twenty-five years of J. Ann Tickner's work and records feminist IR's development from disciplinary margins into sustained debate. It is not a triumphalist history but a reflective logbook: which concepts feminism has reformulated, why mainstream theorists and feminists so often “do not understand” one another, and what questions of race, empire, and method remain when gender analysis seeks to alter disciplinary authority.

The early essays directly reformulate Hans Morgenthau's political realism. Tickner's objection is not simply the absence of women diplomats. Realism's reason, autonomy, power, and state survival draw upon masculinized experience. She widens security beyond territory and military capacity to economic, environmental, and bodily security, treating war, state, and citizenship as gendered institutions. This does not soften IR; it exposes the labor and violence omitted from models of states and markets.

Essays on the world economy and globalization trace women's concentration in low-wage production, informal work, and social reproduction. Open markets depend upon differentiated arrangements of borders, families, and care; states and markets are not separate domains. An ecofeminist perspective further questions endless growth and national competition by connecting security to resources, environment, and the continuity of life. Though written in the 1990s, these pieces illuminate the gendered structure of supply-chain crises.

The second part addresses methodological conflict. Mainstream IR privileges testable hypotheses, causal models, and detached researchers, while much feminist work relies on interpretive, reflexive, and situated epistemologies. For Tickner this is not a simple quantitative/qualitative dispute. The camps disagree about what the world contains, who can know it, and whom research serves. She seeks dialogue without conceding that feminism becomes legitimate only by imitating positivism.

Later essays consider masculinized discourse after 9/11, religious worldviews, and IR's origin stories. Narratives of heroism/cowardice and civilization/barbarism intensified hostility and blamed feminism and critical thought for national weakness. Rational-choice models struggle with religious meaning, for which hermeneutic and reflexive approaches may be better suited. Finally, feminist and postcolonial scholarship retells disciplinary history, showing race and empire to be constitutive of international order rather than external topics.

The volume also reveals historical limits. Earlier essays sometimes contrast “women” and “men” broadly; intersectionality, queer and trans IR, disability, and difference within the global South require later scholarship. Organizing a field's journey around one scholar may foreground an individual genealogy over feminist IR's collective and multiregional origins. Tickner herself describes knowledge production as collective, so the book should be an entry point rather than a complete history.

For FemRes, the book's strength is showing both theoretical claims and the resistance they meet within a discipline. It teaches readers not only to ask how gender affects international politics, but why international politics was defined around statesmen and war, what evidence receives the name rigor, and which histories disappear. The feminist voyage does not end at an established center. It alters the map, methods of navigation, and the people authorized to produce knowledge.
