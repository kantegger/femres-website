Published in 1991, Feminism Confronts Technology asks a question that still anchors technofeminism: will technology liberate women or reinforce the sexual division of labor? Judy Wajcman rejects the choice between technological optimism and technological determinism. Machines do not automatically produce equality, nor do they oppress independently of society. From problem definition and design teams to investment and use, technologies take shape within existing arrangements of gender, class, and work.

She first attacks the narrow definition of technology. Heavy industry, weapons, and engineering are coded as highly skilled masculine domains, while sewing, food processing, care equipment, and office machines become ordinary tools of women's work. This classification naturalizes women's knowledge and then cites their supposed lack of technical competence to justify exclusion. Masculinity is not an incidental culture surrounding technical occupations; it structures which skills receive authority and pay.

Her account of production shows that automation does not neutrally remove drudgery. Management can design machinery to fragment skill, intensify surveillance, and concentrate women in poorly paid jobs, or technology can support more autonomous work. The feminist question is not only how many women enter engineering, but who controls production, whose experience enters design, and who bears displacement and repetition. This is an important prehistory to research on digital labor and algorithmic management.

In the home, a labor-saving appliance may reduce one physical task while raising standards of cleanliness, expanding consumption, and leaving women responsible for the whole system of housework. Saved minutes do not become freedom when household divisions and social expectations remain unchanged. Wajcman consequently makes washing machines, food processors, and housing design political: infrastructure allocates the coordination of everyday life, and the private household is part of a technical system.

Reproductive and medical technologies most directly concern bodily autonomy. They can widen choices in contraception, reproduction, and diagnosis, yet also place women's bodies under male-dominated professions, commercial markets, and state population policies. The task is not a blanket endorsement or rejection but analysis of consent, risk, access, and control. Her chapter on the built environment likewise shows how transport, lighting, housing, and workplace location encode conflicts between paid work and care.

The book bears the limits of its period. The internet, platform capitalism, algorithmic discrimination, nonbinary gender, and global electronics supply chains are not central, and the analysis largely begins from British and Australian industrial societies. “Women” and “men” can appear too coherent, with race, colonialism, disability, and sexuality underdeveloped. Those absences do not invalidate the framework; they mark the work later pursued by data feminism, design justice, and decolonial technology studies.

For FemRes readers, its greatest value is the method of social shaping, not a verdict on obsolete machines. Confronted with AI, smart homes, or reproductive technology, we should ask who defined the problem, whose data and bodies become inputs, who receives technical authority, and who performs invisible maintenance. Adding women users or engineers is insufficient by itself. A feminist politics of technology must redistribute design power, reorganize labor, and alter who carries risk and receives benefit.
