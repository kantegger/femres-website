In this episode of “How to Be a Better Human,” Ai-jen Poo begins with a simple reversal: the people who care for children, older adults, disabled people, and homes should not have to sacrifice their own security in order to make everyone else’s life possible. Speaking from decades of domestic-worker organizing, she explains how care is treated as a private family matter even though nearly every household will need it and the broader economy cannot function without it. Low pay and invisibility are therefore designed features of a system, not evidence that care requires little skill.

The conversation links everyday employment relationships to a larger care infrastructure. A family may genuinely love a nanny or home-care worker and still participate in arrangements shaped by immigration status, race, gender, weak labor protections, and scarce public support. Poo asks listeners to begin conversations before crisis arrives: what care will loved ones need, who will provide it, what will make that job sustainable, and which costs should be shared through policy rather than pushed onto women inside individual households?

The episode is not a technical blueprint for funding long-term care, enforcing labor standards, or resolving every tension between affordability and decent work. Its contribution is to make those policy questions intimate without privatizing them. For feminist readers, it connects care ethics to wages, paid leave, worker voice, disability and aging justice, and the right both to receive care and to give it under dignified conditions. Paired with Poo’s shorter TEDWomen talk, it also shows the development of a public argument: appreciation becomes meaningful only when it is converted into rights, resources, and durable institutions.
