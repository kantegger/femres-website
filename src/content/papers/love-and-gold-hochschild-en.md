Arlie Russell Hochschild’s “Love and Gold” makes the global care chain visible through the life of Vicky Diaz, a Filipina mother who leaves her own children to work as a housekeeper and caregiver in wealthy Los Angeles households. The essay asks readers to follow not only money sent home but also affection, attention, and maternal labor moving in the opposite direction. Like older global commodity chains that transferred gold and raw materials from poorer regions to richer ones, the contemporary care economy draws an intimate resource from families whose economic options have already been narrowed.

Hochschild’s argument is not that migrant mothers love their children less. It shows the costly strategies through which love is stretched across distance: remittances, calls, gifts, substitute caregivers, disciplined emotion, and hopes for reunion. Employers may experience a worker’s devotion as a personal virtue while remaining insulated from the immigration regimes, wage inequalities, and public-care shortages that make it available. The resulting “care drain” links private feeling to global political economy and unsettles the comforting idea that one family’s loving arrangement can be judged apart from the conditions surrounding it.

The chain metaphor is powerful but should not flatten migrant women into victims or treat care as a fixed substance simply removed from one child and delivered to another. Families improvise, children and caregivers interpret separation differently, and migrant workers exercise agency within constrained choices. Read alongside research by Rhacel Salazar Parreñas and migrant domestic workers’ own organizing, the essay remains a foundational introduction to transnational feminist care analysis. It teaches readers to ask where emotional abundance comes from, whose absence supports it, and why just care requires labor rights, migration justice, and public responsibility across borders rather than private gratitude alone.
