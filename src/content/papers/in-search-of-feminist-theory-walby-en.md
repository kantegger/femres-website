Sylvia Walby’s “In search of feminist theory” replies to commentators on her earlier intervention about politics of location and the power of argument. The article opens with a disciplinary challenge: analyses of politics are not automatically theories, and feminist scholarship still needs concepts capable of explaining patterned relations across cases. Walby also identifies a possible convergence in the debate—difference cannot be understood without attention to overarching frameworks and overlaps, while social locations should not be treated as wholly incommensurable.

Her position defends comparison and generalization without requiring that every woman share the same experience. Theory, in this account, should specify mechanisms, relations, and scales that allow claims to be assessed beyond one location. The global context makes this need more urgent because institutions, labor markets, states, and movements cross borders. Yet the exchange also exposes the risk: a framework broad enough to travel may erase the histories and power relations that make locations unequal. Walby’s answer is to seek stronger explanation rather than abandon cross-context argument.

The article is most illuminating when read as one part of a live controversy, not as the final definition of feminist theory. Phoenix presses the politics of alliance, Knapp examines the architecture and terms of Walby’s argument, and Felski considers reason and narrative. Their disagreement clarifies what is at stake in the word “theory”: explanatory reach, accountability to difference, and the standards by which claims can be criticized. For FemRes readers, the exchange offers a model of feminism that treats argument as collective work rather than a demand for premature consensus.
