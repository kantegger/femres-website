Douglas questions histories in which feminism’s “first wave” belongs to Europe and North America while Arab women appear later as recipients of an imported politics. Through journals, salons, letters, translation, and organizing across Egypt, Palestine, and Lebanon, the article reconstructs a field of intellectual and activist exchange that was both locally rooted and transnational.

Networks matter because they change the unit of history. A national biography can make a woman look exceptional and isolated; correspondence and periodicals reveal arguments, mentorship, disagreement, and circulation. They also show Arab feminists engaging Ottoman reform, colonial rule, nationalism, education, labor, and suffrage on terms not reducible to Western influence.

Adding Arab pioneers to an unchanged wave chronology would not be enough. Douglas uses the archive to question who defined the wave, which languages counted as theory, and why some connections disappeared from feminist memory. The article offers a decolonial historiographic practice: recover relation, not merely famous names, and let those relations alter the map of feminism itself.
