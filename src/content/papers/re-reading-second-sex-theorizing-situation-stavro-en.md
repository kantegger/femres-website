Elaine Stavro’s “Re-reading the Second Sex” argues against the familiar claim that Simone de Beauvoir merely applied Jean-Paul Sartre’s existentialism to “the woman question.” According to the published abstract, Beauvoir uses Sartre’s universal categories of Being and Nothingness transgressively, bringing them into contact with the economic, political, cultural, and psychological conditions of women’s lives. This move gives Beauvoir an independent philosophical project rather than casting her as Sartre’s derivative companion.

The article centers the concept of situation. Sartrean radical freedom risks becoming abstract and voluntarist if consciousness is imagined as able to transcend circumstances through choice alone. Beauvoir’s account of situated freedom makes embodiment and social conditions part of what freedom means and what possibilities are practically available. Stavro therefore reads *The Second Sex* as a transformation of existentialism: it concretizes an abstract philosophical voice and challenges the elevation of masculine experience and disembodied consciousness as universal.

Stavro also places Beauvoir closer to Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s existential attention to embodiment than many standard readings allow. This does not make *The Second Sex* immune from criticism, but it changes the terms on which it should be evaluated. For feminist philosophy, the essay’s enduring contribution is methodological as well as historical: intellectual influence need not equal dependence, and a thinker can inhabit inherited concepts while altering their force. Readers interested in agency and structure, body and freedom, or the gendering of philosophical canons will find this a focused route back into Beauvoir’s own argument.
