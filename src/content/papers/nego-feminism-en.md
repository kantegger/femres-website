Nnaemeka’s ‘nego-feminism’ carries two linked meanings: feminism through negotiation and a feminism practiced without the ego of a single universal model. Drawing on African social and literary contexts, she argues that women often pursue change through compromise, coalition, evasion, reinterpretation, and carefully chosen confrontation. These strategies should not automatically be read as false consciousness or insufficient radicalism.

The article challenges theories that recognize agency only when it resembles direct opposition. Negotiation can reveal a precise understanding of where power is located and which relationships must be preserved, altered, or refused. Yet the concept is not a celebration of accommodation. Its feminist value depends on whether negotiation changes the distribution of power and expands women’s capacity to act.

Nego-feminism has been influential because it names context-sensitive politics without rejecting transnational solidarity. It also invites criticism: appeals to cultural harmony can be used to discipline anger or protect abusive institutions. The framework is strongest when treated as an analytic question—what does this strategy make possible, for whom, and at what cost?—rather than a cultural prescription for how African women should resist.
