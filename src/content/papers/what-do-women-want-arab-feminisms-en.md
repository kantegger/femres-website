Hatem treats the question “what do women want?” as a problem of political representation rather than a request for one list. Arab feminist agendas have been shaped by nationalist projects, state institutions, leftist and Islamist movements, professional organizations, donor frameworks, and autonomous organizing. Each creates different openings and different limits on who can speak.

The article’s mapping resists a progress story in which an older feminism simply yields to a better new generation. State feminism may produce education or legal reform while disciplining independent politics; transnational funding may expand capacity while reorganizing accountability; younger movements may innovate while losing institutional memory. “Arab feminisms” in the plural names these uneven histories.

Its regional scale necessarily abstracts from local struggles, and political developments after 2013 require substantial updating. Still, the paper provides a rigorous question for every feminist platform: what institutions translate a demand into “women’s interests,” which women are absent from that translation, and how can coalitions remain answerable to disagreement rather than covering it with unity?
