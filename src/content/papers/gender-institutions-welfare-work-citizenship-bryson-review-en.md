Lois Bryson’s short review considers *Gender and Institutions: Welfare, Work and Citizenship*, the 1998 collection edited by Moira Gatens and Alison Mackinnon. The book’s three terms are analytically connected: welfare systems distribute support and dependency, workplaces organize paid and unpaid labor, and citizenship defines whose contributions and claims receive public recognition. A feminist institutional approach asks how these arrangements produce gendered outcomes even when their rules appear formally neutral.

The value of Bryson’s review lies partly in its historical location. Published in 2000, it records how an Australian feminist scholar assessed a collection concerned with institutional change at the end of a period marked by welfare restructuring and debates over women’s relation to the state. Rather than treating inequality as the sum of individual prejudice, the object under review directs attention to policy categories, employment norms, care responsibilities, and definitions of membership. “Women” cannot simply be inserted into institutions whose underlying model of worker, citizen, or dependent was already gendered.

This entry should be read according to its genre: it is a book review, not a substitute for the edited collection and not an independent empirical study. Its usefulness is orientation—identifying an institutional vocabulary and pointing readers toward Gatens and Mackinnon’s volume. For contemporary readers, the central methodological lesson remains strong: reform must examine how welfare, work, and citizenship reinforce one another. Changing one rule may have limited effect if unpaid care, labor-market insecurity, and unequal access to public voice continue to be organized elsewhere.
