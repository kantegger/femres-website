Tamale rejects the singular phrase ‘African sexuality’ as if a continent possessed one stable intimate culture. Colonial administrations, missionary Christianity, reformist religious movements, postcolonial law, medicine, and nationalism all participated in classifying legitimate and deviant sex. Norms now defended as ancient tradition often carry the institutional history of colonial rule.

The article shows how law and religion reinforce each other while claiming different authority. Criminal codes police same-sex intimacy and reproductive autonomy; political leaders invoke culture to consolidate legitimacy; and moral campaigns make women and queer people symbolic boundaries of the nation. Human-rights arguments are necessary but can fail when they ignore this history or appear only as external instruction.

A decolonial feminist response must therefore do more than oppose culture to universal rights. It must recover suppressed histories, support local movements, analyze material power, and defend people whose lives do not fit official narratives. Tamale’s contribution is to make sexuality central to African political analysis rather than an embarrassing private topic imported by outsiders.
