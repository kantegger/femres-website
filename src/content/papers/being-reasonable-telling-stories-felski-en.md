Rita Felski’s “Being reasonable, telling stories” enters the same *Feminist Theory* exchange over argument, location, and the possibility of shared claims. Its paired terms resist a familiar choice. Feminism has good reasons to distrust a disembodied “Reason” historically coded as masculine and universal, yet stories and experience do not automatically explain themselves or settle political disagreement. Felski asks what each form can do rather than treating theory and narrative as enemies.

Narrative gives feminist knowledge texture: it carries sequence, motive, contradiction, affect, and the specificity of a life that an abstract category can erase. Argument performs different work by making assumptions explicit, connecting cases, inviting challenge, and testing whether a conclusion follows from its evidence. Either mode can dominate or exclude. A story can be granted false immunity from criticism because it is authentic; an argument can disguise a privileged standpoint as neutral procedure. Their combination allows experience to trouble a concept and concepts to reveal patterns no single account can establish.

The essay remains valuable for reading testimony, memoir, criticism, and movement speech without forcing them into a hierarchy. It does not imply that one compelling story proves a universal claim, or that public reason is free of power. Instead, it offers a practice of feminist interpretation in which stories can generate and revise arguments, while arguments remain answerable to lives they seek to describe. Read alongside Walby, Phoenix, and Knapp, Felski’s contribution also shows why disagreement over style is political: the accepted form of knowledge helps determine who can enter a theoretical conversation.
