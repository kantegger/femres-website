Bronwyn Winter’s “Who Counts (or Doesn’t Count) What as Feminist Theory?” uses dictionary work to make a disciplinary question concrete. Dictionaries and reference works do more than report an already settled field: by selecting entries, naming traditions, and arranging relations among concepts, they help construct the canon they appear merely to describe. The parenthetical phrase “or doesn’t count” keeps exclusions visible and turns ordinary reference use into an exercise in knowledge politics.

This approach encourages readers to inspect the material devices through which theory becomes authoritative. Whose names recur? Which terms receive their own entries and which appear only under another heading? What linguistic, geographical, disciplinary, or institutional limits structure the selection? Absence alone does not prove a deliberate exclusion, and no reference work can include everything. The critical task is to compare editorial principles with their effects rather than treating claims of comprehensiveness or neutrality as self-validating.

The source record offers no public abstract, so this revised entry avoids the earlier catalogue of allegedly included and excluded schools, which could not be checked against the cited article. Its secure value is methodological: Winter turns the boundary of feminist theory into something readers can investigate rather than accept. Paired with Ermarth’s “What Counts as Feminist Theory?”, the essay helps students examine syllabi, indexes, encyclopedias, databases, and search systems as infrastructures of recognition. Feminist epistemology here begins with a practical question: not only what knowledge says, but how it is selected, classified, and made findable.
