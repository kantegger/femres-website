Glas and Alexander begin from a category often obscured in survey research: people who identify strongly with Islam while supporting expanded rights for women. By analyzing fifty-one surveys and roughly 57,000 respondents, they ask which forms of religiosity and which social contexts are associated with what they call Muslim feminism.

The study distinguishes religious commitment from interpretations and institutions that enforce hierarchy. This matters empirically and politically. A single scale that treats greater religiosity as automatically less feminist can build its conclusion into the measurement and misrecognize reform movements that argue from within Islamic traditions.

Large comparative datasets reveal patterns but cannot fully capture what respondents mean by equality, Islam, or feminism, and national surveys may exclude precarious populations. The paper is most useful as a corrective to civilizational assumptions and as an invitation to better measurement. It shows that faith and gender justice are not fixed opposites; their relation is made through education, authority, interpretation, and political context.
