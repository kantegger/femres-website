Mhajne and Brandt compare Islamist women’s activism in Tunisia and Egypt after the 2011 uprisings. The two transitions created different institutional opportunities, party strategies, and levels of repression. Those differences shaped how women activists framed democracy, family, religious authority, and their own place inside political organizations.

The comparison complicates the habit of classifying Islamist women as either emancipated actors or instruments of male-led parties. Participation can expand public authority while leaving internal gender hierarchy intact; rights language can be sincerely used and strategically bounded at the same time. Agency and constraint are not mutually exclusive findings.

Because rapid authoritarian change altered both cases, the article is a historically situated account rather than a timeless description of Islamist politics. Its enduring methodological lesson is to study what women do within movements, what organizational resources they control, and which disagreements they can voice. Labels such as secular or Islamist are starting points, not explanations of gender politics.
