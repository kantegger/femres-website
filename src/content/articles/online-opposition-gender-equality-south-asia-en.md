This UN Women brief examines online opposition to gender equality in Bangladesh, India, and the Philippines. Rather than counting only explicit slurs, it identifies recurring narratives: feminism is framed as foreign, anti-family, anti-men, elitist, or incompatible with national and religious identity. That wider frame matters because opposition often travels through jokes, moral concern, and claims to protect culture long before it becomes direct abuse.

The report helps move discussion beyond the idea of a few malicious users. Platform design rewards outrage and repetition; political actors can connect misogyny to nationalism; and influencers can translate structural backlash into intimate anxieties about dating, work, family authority, and masculinity. Women and gender-diverse people then face not only hostile speech but a reduced ability to participate in public life without surveillance or reputational attack.

Its comparative scope is useful but limited: three countries cannot represent Asia, and rapid platform change can date specific examples. Readers should pair the brief with research led by local feminist and queer organizations, especially work attentive to caste, class, language, disability, and rural access. Its lasting contribution is methodological: digital safety requires challenging the narratives and business systems that make misogyny profitable, not merely teaching individual women to endure it more carefully.
