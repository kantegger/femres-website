Amnesty International’s feature centers seven women who participated in Argentina’s Green Wave, the mass campaign symbolized by the green scarf. Their testimonies document how a demand long confined to feminist and health networks became publicly speakable across schools, families, workplaces, streets, and legislative debate. The resource is valuable because it preserves movement voices at a moment when legal change was still uncertain.

The campaign reframed abortion as a question of public health, social inequality, and democratic citizenship rather than private morality. Criminalization does not prevent abortion; it distributes safety according to money, geography, age, and access to information. By linking bodily autonomy to preventable death and unequal care, activists expanded the constituency beyond those already comfortable identifying as feminist.

The article is movement testimony, not a comprehensive history or evaluation. It gives limited space to tensions over trans-inclusive language, regional inequality, Indigenous perspectives, and implementation beyond national legislation. Readers should pair it with later research. Still, it captures a crucial political mechanism: symbols matter when they carry networks, education, mutual support, and a shared language capable of moving between intimate experience and institutional power.
