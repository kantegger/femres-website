This analysis begins from a limitation in familiar gender indicators: the number of women holding office says little about whether institutions redistribute authority, money, and agenda-setting power. It places current governance in a longer history, arguing that colonial and postcolonial state formation marginalized women while erasing or subordinating earlier forms of women’s public authority.

The report examines barriers including political finance, party gatekeeping, violence, care responsibilities, media hostility, and informal networks that determine who can turn a seat into influence. Quotas may open doors, but without protection, resources, and institutional redesign, women representatives can remain isolated or pressured to reproduce existing priorities.

Its feminist value lies in moving from presence to power. The report still works largely within a reform framework and gives less attention to movements that challenge the state from outside. Read critically, it offers a useful diagnostic: representation matters, but only when connected to organized constituencies and material changes in how governance is financed, secured, and made accountable.
