This commentary reads Morocco’s women’s movement through its ability to act together at decisive moments. Campaigns around the 2000 women’s march, the 2004 reform of the Moudawana family code, the 2011 constitution, and the aftermath of Amina Filali’s death show that legal victories rarely come from a single organization or charismatic leader. They depend on durable pressure, a legible demand, and coalitions capable of surviving disagreement.

Bensouda gives particular attention to divisions between secular and Islamic feminist currents, competition over leadership, and changing generational or queer demands. Coalition is not presented as consensus. It is a political technology for deciding what can be pursued together without erasing difference. The article’s concise model can simplify the deeper history of class, monarchy, rural organizing, and state co-optation, but it usefully directs attention from labels to movement capacity.

The larger lesson is that reform is an event and an implementation struggle. A changed statute can rearrange public possibility while courts, resources, and social authority limit its reach. Feminist movements therefore need both recognizable campaigns and organizations that remain after the headline victory—to monitor institutions, broaden constituencies, and contest the meaning of the reform they won.
