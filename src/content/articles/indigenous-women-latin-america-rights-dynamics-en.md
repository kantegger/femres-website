This ECLAC-led report brings demographic evidence into conversation with the human-rights agendas developed by women’s and Indigenous movements. It examines physical autonomy, reproductive rights, education, work, poverty, political participation, and public institutions across Latin America. Its scale makes visible recurring inequalities without treating Indigenous women as a culturally uniform population.

A central problem is statistical invisibility. Census categories, inconsistent self-identification measures, missing rural data, and indicators designed without Indigenous participation determine which harms governments can claim to see. Better data is therefore not a neutral technical improvement: it affects budgets, services, land and reproductive policy, and the ability of communities to hold states accountable.

The report’s policy framework can flatten political differences among nations and peoples, and its 2013 evidence needs updating. Nevertheless, it is a foundational regional map. Read alongside Indigenous women’s own organizations and scholarship, it helps connect bodily autonomy to territory, language, collective rights, economic structure, and representation—dimensions too often separated in mainstream gender policy.
