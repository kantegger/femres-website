UN Women’s policy brief treats women’s movements as political infrastructure. Legal reforms and representation gains rarely appear from institutional goodwill alone; they depend on organizations that create analysis, train leaders, document violence, sustain campaigns, and hold governments accountable. The brief surveys both achievements and the uneven capacity behind them.

Its central concerns include shrinking civic space, short-term donor funding, generational divides, rural and urban inequality, digital harassment, and fragmentation between professional NGOs and grassroots groups. Funding can expand reach while also redirecting priorities toward measurable projects. Movement autonomy therefore requires resources that support long-term organizing, care, security, and institutional memory rather than only campaigns visible to donors.

As an institutional document, the brief sometimes compresses political disagreement into the language of capacity and partnership. Readers should pair it with movement-produced texts such as the African Feminist Charter. Together they reveal a crucial distinction: including women in existing institutions is not the same as building collective power capable of transforming those institutions.
