Amnesty International’s International Women’s Day feature interviews six women human rights defenders from across South Asia: Laila Haidari, Sara Hossain, Fareesha Abdulla, Charimaya Tamang, Veeru Kohli, and Sithi Umma. Their work spans addiction and poverty, civil liberties, trafficking, bonded labor, enforced disappearance, and political accountability.

Read together, the interviews resist a single story of ‘South Asian women.’ The activists occupy different legal systems, religions, classes, ethnic positions, and relationships to the state. What connects them is not an essential regional identity but the gendered form retaliation takes: sexualized defamation, threats to family, accusations of immorality, and the expectation that women should serve communities without claiming political authority.

The short question-and-answer format cannot provide a full history of each struggle, and an international NGO’s framing may smooth ideological differences. It is best used as a map leading to the organizations and local archives around each defender. Its feminist value lies in expanding the meaning of women’s rights work: defending democracy, minorities, disappeared people, and economic survival is not separate from feminism when gender determines who may speak, whose grief becomes public, and who is punished for demanding accountability.
