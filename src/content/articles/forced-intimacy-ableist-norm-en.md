Mia Mingus uses “forced intimacy” to name a routine condition of disabled life: access is often granted only after a person discloses diagnoses, pain, bodily functions, trauma, or private needs to strangers and authorities. The coercion can also be physical, as when someone moves a wheelchair without consent or when assistance requires unwanted touch. Mingus distinguishes this from access intimacy—the trust that grows when another person understands and responds to access needs without making the disabled person plead, educate, or perform gratitude. What institutions describe as neutral procedure is therefore an unequal distribution of vulnerability.

The essay expands feminist accounts of consent beyond sex and partnership. Schools, hospitals, workplaces, airports, families, and activist events can all make survival depend on compulsory exposure. Mingus shows how ableism intersects with race, gender, class, adoption, immigration, and sexuality: the person who must “prove” a need is also judged for whether they seem credible, pleasant, and deserving of help. Even well-intentioned accommodation can reproduce domination when nondisabled people control the terms, assume any help should be accepted, or withhold access from someone they dislike.

Its political force lies in refusing the choice between isolated independence and coerced dependence. Mingus does not reject interdependence; she argues that access contains a potentially transformative vulnerability when it is built through consent, competence, and trust. For feminist readers, the essay offers a demanding test for care ethics: assistance is not just a logistical outcome, but a relationship in which both bodily autonomy and collective responsibility matter. It is especially useful for redesigning intake forms, medical encounters, events, and organizing cultures so that people receive concrete access information and meaningful choice before being asked to reveal themselves.
