This article follows Kenya’s work toward a National Care Policy. Time-use evidence indicates that Kenyan women spend roughly four to five hours a day on unpaid care, compared with about one hour for men. The gap does not mean only greater fatigue. It restricts stable employment, education, political participation, and rest. Bringing time into policy makes costs long absorbed by households visible within a national development agenda.

The policy’s promise lies in crossing departmental boundaries. Childcare, support for older and disabled people, water, sanitation, energy, transport, paid leave, and care-worker protections are usually administered separately, but they form one time structure in a woman’s day. A nearby water source, affordable childcare, and protected maternity leave may jointly determine whether someone keeps a job; they are not unrelated minor projects.

Freeing women for paid work cannot be the only justification. Care has value in itself, and the dignity of recipients must coexist with workers’ rights. Otherwise unpaid household work may simply be transferred to poorer women. Rural women, informal workers, disabled people, care workers, and service users need authority in policy design so that urban elites do not define everyone’s needs.

Kenya’s case remains between policy formation and implementation, so the article offers a direction rather than an outcome evaluation. Its importance is to show an African state building a care framework through local evidence, organizing, and public institutions rather than merely importing external best practice. The real tests will be budgets, enforcement, coverage, and accountability—not whether the final document uses the language of gender equality.
