So-young, the protagonist of *The Bacchus Lady*, is a sixty-five-year-old “Bacchus lady,” one of the women selling sexual services to older men in Seoul’s Jongno Park, a name associated with the energy drinks used to approach clients. The film treats this occupation not as exotic backdrop but within South Korea’s rapid aging, inadequate pensions, loosening family ties, and women’s lower lifetime earnings. So-young’s body is at once an instrument of survival, a stigmatized body, and a site where others seek touch and care.

Youn Yuh-jung refuses to reduce So-young to victimhood or noble suffering. She is pragmatic, funny, able to negotiate, and willing to care for a Kopino child she encounters by chance. Alongside a transgender neighbor and a disabled prosthetics maker, she forms a marginal community outside the conventional family. They offer one another food, shelter, and companionship, but none possesses adequate institutional security. Mutual aid is real and valuable while also being forced to fill the holes left by public care.

The story turns toward So-young’s older clients. Men living with stroke, dementia, loneliness, and lost control ask her to assist their deaths. Sexual service and end-of-life care enter one concealed chain of labor: society refuses both older people’s desire and their dependency, so its most intimate and dangerous tasks fall to a poor older woman. She has no medical authority or legal protection, yet is asked to carry ethical decisions institutions will not assume.

A feminist reading must hold two points together. Sex work cannot be equated with passive victimhood; So-young has judgment, skill, and relationships with clients. But choice occurs under sharply limited pension, housing, and employment options. The film does not develop decriminalization, labor rights, or social security into policy, which is a limitation. It nevertheless shows how ageism and gender intersect so that a woman who has cared across a lifetime still sells bodily and emotional labor in old age.

The assisted-death plot likewise resists an easy answer. It can register response to suffering and autonomy, but it may obscure an earlier question: did these men receive accessibility, long-term care, psychological support, and relationships that made continued life possible? When death appears the only controllable option, the conditions of choice demand scrutiny. That So-young ultimately bears the legal consequence reveals how collective failure is individualized.

*The Bacchus Lady* is a film about labor as much as aging. It makes visible people left in parks, cheap clinics, and small rooms by narratives of Korean modernization, and challenges the claim that families naturally care for elders. Society calls So-young disreputable while she performs the work it most needs and least acknowledges: touch, listening, care, and accompaniment toward death. The answer is not to praise her sacrifice, but to ask why an affluent society makes these responsibilities fall on her in such isolated, dangerous, and unequal form.
