Mira is a high-achieving prefect at a strict boarding school, skilled at inhabiting the authority that the institution temporarily grants her. Her attraction to a new student, Sri, opens a private world of experiment and uncertainty. But school discipline, sexual double standards, and the arrival of her mother Anila make adolescence into a field where curiosity is constantly observed and interpreted by others.

Shuchi Talati centers female desire without presenting it as either danger or automatic liberation. Mira wants knowledge and control, yet romance contains manipulation, embarrassment, and unequal experience. The film’s attention to awkwardness matters because girls on screen are often denied the right to learn gradually: they must be innocent, corrupted, or exceptionally self-aware. Mira is allowed to make judgments and revise them.

The mother-daughter relationship gives the film its most complicated feminist insight. Anila’s interference is partly protective and partly shaped by a youth she was never permitted to have. Her behavior can wound Mira without making her merely jealous or monstrous. Patriarchal restriction does not pass between generations as a simple command; it can return as longing, rivalry, anxiety, and an attempt to participate belatedly in a daughter’s freedom.

The boarding school teaches leadership while policing the body that exercises it. Mira’s development therefore requires more than defying rules or winning a boy. She must distinguish desire from approval and authority from agency. *Girls Will Be Girls* is valuable because it treats coming of age as relational: daughters need boundaries from mothers, mothers need lives beyond daughters, and both need a language for sexuality that is not organized by shame.
