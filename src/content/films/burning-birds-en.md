Set in Sri Lanka during the political violence of the late 1980s, *Burning Birds* follows Kusum after her husband is abducted and killed by paramilitary forces. Left responsible for eight children and an elderly mother-in-law, she moves through a shrinking field of work: cleaning, slaughterhouse labor, and forms of sexual exchange produced by desperation. The film locates war not only in armed confrontation but in the household economy that remains after a man disappears.

Kusum is praised as a devoted mother only while sacrifice remains invisible and sexually respectable. Once survival pushes her beyond approved femininity, the same community that failed to support the family becomes willing to condemn her. This double standard is central: women are made responsible for keeping children alive, yet denied the wages, property, safety, and bodily autonomy required to do so.

The film’s bleakness risks making female suffering appear inexhaustible, and its male-directed gaze warrants scrutiny, especially when sexual violence accumulates. Yet its strongest scenes refuse moral distance. Institutions that might provide justice—the state, employers, schools, religious or community authority—appear as participants in abandonment rather than neutral refuges.

A feminist reading should resist measuring Kusum by whether every decision is admirable. The political question is why her choices become so narrow and why social judgment expands as material support disappears. *Burning Birds* shows the afterlife of conflict as gendered economic violence: women are expected to repair a shattered world with their bodies and then punished for the visible marks of that repair.
