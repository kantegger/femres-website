*Soongava* centers Diya, a classical dancer, and Kiran, the woman she loves. Their relationship becomes publicly dangerous when Diya’s family arranges her marriage to a man and expects obedience to restore social order. By locating lesbian desire within homes, rehearsal spaces, rented rooms, and streets, the film asks what infrastructure is required for love to become a livable life rather than a private exception.

Family opposition is not presented simply as emotional prejudice. Marriage organizes reputation, property, kinship, and women’s economic security. Refusing it therefore threatens a network of obligations that extends beyond the couple. The women’s limited housing and institutional support show why visibility alone cannot guarantee freedom: coming out can be politically important while materially exposing people who lack independent resources.

As an early landmark, the film carries representational burdens no single work can satisfy. Its melodrama and tragic pressures may frustrate viewers seeking queer joy, and it cannot stand for the diversity of Nepali lesbian, bisexual, and trans experience. Yet judging it only against later representational ideals would miss the risk of placing reciprocal love between two women at the center of a national feature film.

For FemRes, *Soongava* is useful as both text and historical event. It makes clear that bodily autonomy requires more than the right to desire: it requires housing, work, chosen kin, legal recognition, and freedom from family violence. The orchid of the title suggests beauty under cultivated conditions; the film’s harder question is who controls those conditions and whether queer women are allowed to cultivate a future of their own.
