Fausta believes she carries “the milk of sorrow,” fear transmitted through the breast milk of mothers raped during Peru’s internal armed conflict. The premise makes historical violence bodily and intergenerational without presenting trauma as a memory that can simply be narrated and left behind. After her mother dies, funeral costs and work in a wealthy household force Fausta into encounters that test the defenses she has built around her body.

The film links sexual violence to class and racial hierarchy. Fausta’s employer, a pianist, hears the songs through which Fausta holds family memory and turns them into cultural material for her own career. Domestic service becomes a site where intimacy and exploitation coexist: the employer can admire Indigenous expression while disregarding the woman who produces it.

Claudia Llosa’s symbolic style has generated important debate. The film can be read as giving visual and musical form to violence that official narratives suppress, but it can also risk making an Indigenous woman’s body mysterious, pathological, or available to an art-house gaze. Feminist viewing should keep that tension open rather than treating the film’s awards as proof that representation is politically resolved.

Fausta’s movement toward agency is quiet and incomplete. Healing does not mean forgetting the war or accepting other people’s access to her body; it involves creating conditions in which fear no longer dictates every relation. The film is useful for discussing wartime rape, inherited trauma, reproductive autonomy, domestic labor, and the unequal circulation of Indigenous women’s knowledge.
