Set in the 1980s, 3000 Nights follows Layal, a newly married Palestinian teacher falsely accused and sentenced to eight years in an Israeli prison. When she discovers she is pregnant, officials pressure her to abort and to inform on other Palestinian prisoners. She gives birth while shackled and attempts to raise her son inside a system designed to control time, movement, kinship, and political allegiance.

Motherhood here is neither private refuge nor sentimental proof of innocence. The prison administration uses the child as leverage, while the women around Layal turn care into collective infrastructure. Feeding, watching, teaching, and protecting the baby become shared practices through which prisoners preserve social life. The film refuses the fantasy of the self-sufficient heroic mother; survival is organized.

Masri also stages difficult relations among Palestinian political prisoners, Israeli criminal prisoners, and prison authorities. The institution encourages hierarchy and conflict, but common vulnerability does not erase unequal histories of occupation. Layal’s political consciousness develops through these contradictions and through a hunger strike that makes bodily dependence a form of collective pressure.

The film condenses testimonies and historical experiences into fiction, so it should be read alongside accounts by former prisoners rather than as a complete record. Its feminist force lies in showing incarceration as an attack on reproduction, intimacy, and futurity as well as liberty. Layal’s resistance is not simply endurance; it is a struggle over whether prison power can determine the meaning of motherhood and the horizon of her child’s life.
