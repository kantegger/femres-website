*The Second Mother* is a restrained domestic drama that makes global labor chains visible inside one apartment block. Val’s life appears to be made of routine tasks—cooking, cleaning, caring for a child—yet those routines are the material through which class hierarchy is produced and repeated every day. The film is strongest where it shows no dramatic break from the start: care can look stable while the person providing it remains structurally expendable.

Before Jéssica arrives, the home’s hierarchy already functions as a choreography of permission. Val is asked to be reliable without ever being treated as a full member of the household she keeps running. She does the emotional and logistical work that makes the employers’ modern professional lives possible, but she is still measured against an unspoken contract: she is needed, yet never equal. This contradiction is why the film’s tension is so high without a single “big” confrontation; the political weight accumulates in who enters the kitchen, who sits at the table, and who owns the time to be idle.

From a feminist perspective, the film links maternal experience to labor rights in an unusually sharp way. Val’s separation from her own daughter is not only an emotional wound; it is an institutional design that shifts care work from one woman’s family to another’s convenience. Anna Muylaert avoids moralizing and instead asks a structural question: how can a society treat women’s reproductive and care labor as abundant while still organizing it as disposable service? The answer remains in the home’s architecture, where warmth and exclusion are administered together.

Premiered at Sundance in 2015, the film later traveled through the Berlin Panorama and was Brazil’s submission to the 88th Academy Awards. Its recognition at the Sundance Special Jury Prize for Acting and Berlin Audience Award matters less than its practical use in feminist discourse: it gives us a precise cinematic grammar for thinking about domestic workers, transgenerational mothering, and classed intimacy—not as isolated themes, but as one system that reproduces inequality from inside “private” space.
