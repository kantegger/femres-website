In Moolaadé, four girls flee a ceremony of genital cutting and ask Collé Ardo for protection. Collé invokes moolaadé, a recognized form of sanctuary, stretching a colored rope across her doorway. The film’s central conflict is therefore not a simple opposition between modernity and an undifferentiated ‘tradition.’ One customary authority is used to resist another, exposing culture as a field of interpretation, power, and struggle.

Sembène gives unusual attention to divisions among women. Mothers, cutters, elders, and younger girls occupy different positions; some defend the ceremony because social respectability and marriageability depend on it, while others carry memories of pain and loss. Collé’s courage matters, but collective change requires women to recognize that private suffering is politically organized. The sanctuary gradually becomes a public challenge to male authority.

Radios form a second visual and political thread. Men confiscate them because outside information enables comparison and dissent, yet the final images suggest that communication cannot be permanently contained. The film does not celebrate technology by itself. It links access to knowledge with local organizing, embodied testimony, and the capacity to name a practice as violence even when powerful people call it identity.

A serious feminist reading should also note that this story of women’s resistance was written and directed by a man, and that international reception can reduce African societies to a single harmful practice. The film works against that reduction through humor, argument, architecture, and attention to political economy. Its village is neither timeless nor isolated; migration, commerce, media, religion, and postcolonial authority are already present.

Moolaadé endures because it asks how protection becomes solidarity. Collé does not rescue passive girls from outside their world. She creates a temporary boundary within it, and others decide whether to defend that boundary together. Bodily autonomy emerges as a collective institution rather than an abstract individual possession.
