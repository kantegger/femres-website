Beauty and the Dogs follows Mariam, a university student raped by policemen after a party, through the hours in which she tries to obtain medical care and file a complaint. The film unfolds in nine long sequences. Its duration turns bureaucracy into suspense: every corridor, form, locked door, and demand to repeat the story becomes another site where institutional power can exhaust a survivor.

The perpetrators’ police status collapses the distinction between seeking justice and returning to danger. Hospital staff invoke procedure, officers question Mariam’s clothing and conduct, and documentation is withheld because the system protects itself. The film shows rape culture not as a collection of cruel attitudes but as an administrative arrangement that distributes credibility and delay.

Mariam’s persistence is powerful, yet the film does not pretend that individual courage repairs an institution. Youssef’s support helps her move through the night, but he cannot speak in her place, and his political confidence sometimes contrasts with her embodied risk. The camera remains close enough to register fear without making sexual violence into spectacle.

Inspired by a 2012 case in Tunisia, the film belongs to the post-revolutionary question of what political change means when police impunity and gendered shame survive. Its final assertion of dignity is deliberately provisional. A complaint can be an act of resistance, but justice requires transforming the institutions that make reporting another punishment.
