*Plan 75* takes place in a Japan only half a step removed from the present. In response to population aging and fiscal pressure, the government introduces a program encouraging people over seventy-five to accept an assisted death, complete with counseling, administration, and a final spending allowance. Chie Hayakawa avoids a dystopia of spectacular technological violence. Horror instead arrives through courteous language, neat forms, friendly telephone calls, and standardized service. A state need not openly hate older people; once it repeatedly describes them as a cost, death can be repackaged as a mature contribution to society.

Chieko Baisho plays Michi Kakutani, a seventy-eight-year-old woman still supporting herself through cleaning work. After losing her job, she encounters no explicit order to die, only a sequence of narrowing possibilities: employers reject her, housing and public support are inadequate, acquaintances disappear, and the only institution actively reaching out is Plan 75. The film’s central question follows: when the material conditions for living have been withdrawn, how autonomous can a choice to die be? Choice is made not only at the signature but by all the routes closed beforehand.

Gender intensifies this condition. Older women often reach later life with lower lifetime earnings, careers interrupted by care, smaller pensions, and greater longevity, leaving them more exposed to solitary poverty and unmet need. The film does not recite those statistics. Michi’s body, room, uniform, and careful spending register a lifetime’s accumulated economic vulnerability. She is not an abstract demographic burden but a person who has worked, cared, formed friendships, and retained desire. Policy language compresses that history into an age threshold and a budget problem.

The film also follows a young recruitment officer, a female employee hired to make final companionship calls, and a Filipino migrant worker handling clients’ possessions. This dispersed structure refuses the comfort of a single evil bureaucrat. Younger workers are themselves precarious, trained to use empathy while administering a cruel institution; migrants absorb the bodily aftermath society refuses to see. Care has not vanished. It has been broken into scripted, low-paid, outsourced tasks whose gentle service language conceals the withdrawal of public responsibility.

The film’s restraint is both limitation and strength. It gives relatively little space to disability activism, older people’s organizing, or collective alternatives to family care, so resistance can appear private. Yet it neither reduces Michi to a victim nor turns survival into inspirational triumph. *Plan 75* identifies a modern form of violence: the state does not directly force death; it first makes certain lives difficult to afford and then markets exit as dignity. A feminist response must therefore defend more than abstract choice. It must build the housing, income, health care, companionship, and care systems that make choosing to live materially possible.
