*Happy Hour* begins with a friendship that appears settled. Akari, Sakurako, Fumi and Jun live in Kobe, are nearing forty, know one another's habits, and believe they can talk about anything. That confidence breaks when Jun reveals that she is pursuing a contested divorce. The shock is not only that she kept her marriage secret; it is the discovery that intimacy can coexist with profound ignorance. The film then moves through the four women's homes, workplaces and desires, allowing one disclosure to trigger a wider reassessment of the roles each has learned to perform.

The running time—five hours and seventeen minutes—is not a spectacle of scale but the film's central ethical decision. Hamaguchi retains the duration of a communication workshop, drinks afterward, a spa trip, a divorce hearing, a literary reading and its awkward question period. Viewers must sit through pauses, social niceties, failed formulations and revisions rather than receiving only plot-efficient information. Where conventional drama compresses women's daily experience into a marital crisis or a moment of empowerment, *Happy Hour* gives uncertainty time to exist before it becomes a conclusion. Watching becomes an exercise in attention: meaning lies not only in what the women declare, but in how they hesitate, avoid, listen and mishear.

The bodily communication workshop near the beginning supplies the film's method. Participants search for a “centre of balance,” press their foreheads together and attempt to sense another person through the body. The exercises can look comic, but their question is serious: proximity and speech do not guarantee understanding. Four friends can share decades of history and still fail to perceive the marriage in which Jun has been living. Husbands can believe they have fulfilled their duties and remain unable to recognise their wives' loneliness. Intimacy is presented not as transparent access to another self, but as interpretive labour that must be practised and can always fail.

The film's feminist critique of marriage is especially sharp because it rarely supplies a simple villain. Power accumulates through repeated, ordinary arrangements: a husband comes home expecting food and emotional accommodation; a wife's employment and desire are treated as the flexible parts of family life; a divorce court uses apparently neutral procedure to test whether Jun is reasonable, credible and compliant enough to leave. The men are often bewildered by the consequences because they understand unhappiness as a feeling to be managed, not as knowledge that might justify structural change. *Happy Hour* shows how patriarchy survives through normality, respectability and good intentions—and why the emotional labour required to absorb those conditions is exhausting.

Nor does the film idealise female friendship as an automatically safe refuge. Akari's anger, Sakurako's empathy and Fumi's restraint all contain genuine care, but also judgement, possessiveness and projection. When one woman withdraws, the others confront how much they depended on the group to stabilise their own lives. This imperfection is central to the film's feminist value. Solidarity is not unanimity or permanent rescue; it is a difficult practice of renegotiating boundaries, admitting what one does not know, and leaving room for another woman's decision even when it unsettles the group.

The film grew out of a citizen acting workshop in Kobe, and its four leads had little professional acting experience. Hamaguchi rehearsed with the performers over an extended period and continued revising the screenplay during an eight-month shoot, giving the pauses and reactions an unusual material weight. The four actors jointly received Locarno's Best Actress prize, while the screenplay earned a Special Mention. The project remains a male director's framing of women's experience and concentrates largely on urban, middle-class Japanese lives; its length is also a real access barrier. Yet few films so seriously return time to “ordinary” women, making intimacy, care, marriage and self-revision the social structures of the film rather than background to a more important plot.
