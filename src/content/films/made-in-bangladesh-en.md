In *Made in Bangladesh*, Shimu works long hours in a Dhaka garment factory producing clothes for distant consumers. After a co-worker dies in a factory fire, she begins organizing a union with other women. Rubaiyat Hossain grounds the story in procedures—collecting signatures, learning labor law, meeting organizers, and navigating intimidation—so collective power emerges as difficult organizational work rather than sudden heroic awakening.

The factory and household are not separate worlds. Shimu’s wages support domestic life, yet her husband claims authority over how she works and moves. Management likewise depends upon gendered assumptions that young women will be compliant, replaceable, and constrained by family reputation. The film shows how global supply chains profit from this overlap: low wages are possible because households and communities absorb the cost of reproducing labor.

Unionization creates solidarity but not purity. Workers disagree, fear dismissal, and face pressure from supervisors who are themselves positioned between owners and the shop floor. Shimu also learns to perform respectability for institutions that may recognize a petition only when women translate urgent needs into bureaucratic form. These tensions keep the film from treating empowerment as an individual confidence exercise.

For feminist viewers in consumer markets, the film redirects attention from ethical shopping as personal virtue to workers’ capacity to bargain collectively. Shimu does not need rescue by a benevolent brand; she needs enforceable rights, independent organization, and freedom from violence at work and home. The label ‘made in Bangladesh’ becomes a demand to see the political lives of the women whose labor makes the commodity possible.
