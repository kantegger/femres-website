Set inside a multigenerational Lahore household, *Joyland* begins with a familiar patriarchal demand: the family needs a grandson to continue its line. Haider, unemployed and treated as insufficiently masculine, secretly takes a job in an erotic dance theatre and becomes fascinated by Biba, an ambitious trans performer. Yet the film refuses to reduce their encounter to a liberating romance. Haider’s wife Mumtaz, his sister-in-law Nucchi, and Biba each inhabit a different bargain with respectability, work, marriage, and survival.

The household functions as a gender system rather than a merely oppressive father. Men receive authority but are also disciplined by masculinity; women’s paid labor is negotiable, while their reproductive and care labor is presumed available. Mumtaz’s loss of work is therefore not a private disappointment. It shows how the family transfers one person’s freedom to another, maintaining harmony by making women absorb the cost of male failure and collective expectation.

Biba’s presence unsettles this order without turning her into a symbol of effortless freedom. She is charismatic, demanding, vulnerable to public violence, and alert to Haider’s fantasies about her. The film gives a trans woman erotic and professional ambition while also showing how cisgender men can seek refuge in trans femininity without confronting the privileges they retain. Its queer politics are strongest when desire exposes hierarchy rather than magically dissolving it.

A serious feminist reading must therefore keep Mumtaz at the center alongside Haider and Biba. *Joyland* asks who is permitted an interior life when kinship is organized around inheritance, obedience, and the appearance of happiness. Its tragedy is not caused by desire itself, but by a household that can imagine rebellion only as secrecy and can preserve continuity only by sacrificing the person whose needs are easiest to disregard.
