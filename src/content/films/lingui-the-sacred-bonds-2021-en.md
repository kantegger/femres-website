Amina survives in N’Djamena by making wire baskets and guarding the fragile respectability available to an unmarried mother. When she learns that her fifteen-year-old daughter Maria is pregnant and wants an abortion, fear initially appears as anger. The procedure is criminalized and expensive, and public knowledge could expose both women to punishment. Reproductive choice is shown not as a private decision but as a problem of money, transport, secrecy, medical safety, and social trust.

The title refers to a form of sacred social connection, and the film builds that idea through women who quietly exchange knowledge and protection. Amina’s sister, a midwife, neighbors, and other women create an infrastructure beneath official institutions. Their solidarity is not sentimental: it is improvised under risk, marked by disagreement, and necessary because law and family authority have failed them.

A parallel story involving a younger girl threatened with genital cutting connects abortion access to a wider politics of bodily control. Haroun avoids presenting either issue as an exotic custom detached from the state. Poverty, religious respectability, male authority, and legal prohibition reinforce one another, while women’s labor absorbs the consequences. The question is not whether the characters belong to their community, but whether belonging must require silence.

The restrained framing and attention to gestures give the women time to think rather than turning their suffering into spectacle. Yet the film’s focus on intimate bonds can leave the structural politics of legal reform at the edge of the frame. That limit is also instructive: clandestine care may save a life, but it should not be mistaken for justice. Lingui shows both the power of mutual aid and the unacceptable conditions that make it necessary.
