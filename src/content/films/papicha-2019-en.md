Set in Algiers in 1997, Papicha follows Nedjma, a university student who designs dresses and sells them in nightclubs. Armed Islamist groups are expanding their control, while posters and threats instruct women how to dress and disappear from public life. Nedjma’s decision to stage a fashion show is not presented as a universal solution to political violence. It is an attempt to keep imagination, friendship, and bodily self-determination alive as the available world contracts.

Clothing carries contradictory meanings throughout the film. The haik becomes material for Nedjma’s designs, allowing her to work with Algerian history rather than oppose ‘tradition’ to Western freedom. At the same time, enforced dress codes make fabric an instrument of surveillance. The feminist question is therefore not whether covering or fashion is inherently liberating, but who controls interpretation, movement, and consequence.

The film’s emotional center is the group of young women around Nedjma. They disagree about migration, marriage, sexuality, and how much risk resistance can justify. Violence is devastating precisely because it attacks the social world they are building together. Meddour shows political terror entering dormitories, buses, bathrooms, and celebrations, dissolving any safe distinction between public conflict and private life.

Papicha’s energy can make individual artistic defiance appear more available than it was to many women during Algeria’s civil conflict, and its fast narrative leaves limited space for differences of class or rural experience. Still, the film refuses both passive victimhood and a simple triumph. Creativity becomes a practice of mourning and continuity: a way to say that fundamentalism cannot have the final authority over women’s bodies or over the memory of their friendships.
