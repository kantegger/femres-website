In a mountain community shaped by poppy cultivation and armed control, mothers cut their daughters’ hair, dig hiding places, and teach them how to disappear. Tatiana Huezo tells the story through Ana and her friends rather than through cartel leaders or spectacular violence. Danger is heard in engines, helicopters, silences, and the abrupt removal of people from everyday life.

This perspective changes the politics of the narco narrative. The film is less interested in criminal personalities than in the gendered labor required to keep girls alive when state protection is absent or compromised. Mothers appear strict because care has been militarized: every rule about clothing, movement, and visibility is an improvised response to the possibility of abduction.

Friendship preserves a field of childhood that violence cannot fully occupy. Games, school, desire, and attention to the landscape coexist with fear. Huezo’s documentary background is visible in the patient observation of faces and environments, yet the beauty of the images should not make the community’s precarity appear natural or timeless. The region is produced by political economy, militarization, and impunity, not by fate.

A feminist reading connects the film’s missing girls to the unequal visibility of rural and Indigenous women, while recognizing that protection can itself restrict daughters’ freedom. The film offers no easy distinction between resistance and survival. Its power lies in showing care as collective strategy and girlhood as a social world with its own knowledge, not merely a prelude to victimization.
