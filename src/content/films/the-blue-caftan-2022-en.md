In the medina of Salé, Mina and Halim run a caftan shop whose hand-sewn work cannot keep pace with demand. Halim has long concealed his desire for men; Mina knows more than they openly say. When they hire the young apprentice Youssef and Mina becomes seriously ill, the marriage’s tacit arrangement is forced into speech, jealousy, care, and unexpected generosity.

The blue caftan is not decorative background. Its slow construction records labor that industrial production renders uneconomic, and the camera’s attention to cloth, thread, and hands gives care a material form. Mina’s shop work and Halim’s embroidery are both skilled practices, complicating a gender order that would divide commerce, craft, beauty, and domestic devotion into fixed roles.

The film avoids treating the wife as an obstacle to queer freedom or the husband’s secrecy as simple betrayal. Mina possesses desire, anger, humor, and agency of her own. As illness changes the terms of their relationship, love becomes less a claim of ownership than a willingness to see another person fully. That tenderness does not erase the social conditions that made concealment necessary.

Touzani’s restraint may seem to soften the danger faced by queer people in Morocco, and the film centers a carefully bounded triangle rather than a wider political community. Yet its quietness is political. It imagines intimacy beyond compulsory heterosexual performance without sacrificing the wife, and it lets a woman’s knowledge transform the ethical possibilities of the household. Freedom arrives not as departure alone, but as a reorganization of care.
