Four Daughters begins from an absence: two of Olfa Hamrouni’s four daughters left Tunisia and joined the Islamic State in Libya. Kaouther Ben Hania does not reconstruct their story through conventional documentary testimony alone. Professional actors play the missing daughters and sometimes Olfa, while the real mother and two younger daughters interrupt, correct, rehearse, and relive family history before the camera.

The device makes representation itself part of the film. Reenactment can give language to memories that have been repeated as accusation or silence, but it can also expose participants to renewed pain. By keeping actors, crew, and negotiation visible, Ben Hania refuses the illusion that a filmmaker simply extracts truth. The women watch themselves being interpreted and regain some power to contest the scene.

The film traces patriarchal violence across scales. An absent and abusive father, Olfa’s harsh discipline, sexual policing, the upheaval after Tunisia’s revolution, and extremist promises of moral certainty do not form one simple chain of blame. Olfa is both survivor and agent of harm; her daughters are neither passive victims nor romantic rebels. This complexity prevents “radicalization” from becoming an explanation that ends inquiry.

Its most feminist achievement is to make intergenerational transmission visible without reducing motherhood to guilt. Love, fear, punishment, humor, and resistance coexist inside the family. The film asks what daughters inherit when a mother’s survival strategies were formed under violence, and whether collective performance can create a different relation to that inheritance. It offers no therapeutic resolution, only a more accountable way of looking.
