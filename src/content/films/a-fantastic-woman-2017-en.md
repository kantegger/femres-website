Marina’s partner Orlando dies suddenly, but the film refuses to let bereavement remain private. Doctors question her, police treat her as a suspect, and Orlando’s family excludes her from the funeral and the home the couple shared. Because Marina is a trans woman, ordinary rituals of grief become checkpoints at which institutions and relatives demand proof that her relationship, body, and identity are legitimate.

Daniela Vega’s performance keeps Marina from becoming a lesson arranged for cisgender spectators. The camera stays with her anger, discipline, desire, work, and moments of imaginative escape. Mirrors, glass, wind, music, and choreographed movement render subjectivity without reducing trans life to medical transition or victimhood. The title’s “fantastic” quality belongs not to exotic difference but to the effort required to remain fully oneself under organized humiliation.

The film emerged from Chilean debates in which legal identity, family authority, and social recognition were being publicly contested. Its feminist value lies in showing that gender violence is administered through mundane procedures as well as spectacular attacks: who may enter a hospital room, attend a funeral, inherit a dog, keep a key, or be believed by an investigator.

A serious reading should still ask what is gained and lost when a cis male director shapes this story and when resilience becomes the dominant arc available to trans protagonists. Yet Vega’s authorship as performer and the film’s commitment to Marina’s interiority resist many familiar traps. It is an accessible entry into trans feminism precisely because it connects recognition to material rights, grief, space, and the freedom to exist beyond explanation.
