Kena and Ziki meet while their fathers compete in a local election. What begins in glances and friendship becomes a romance lived under the surveillance of family, church, neighborhood gossip, and political respectability. Rafiki refuses the common cinematic demand that queer African lives appear only as evidence of persecution. Its bright colors, pop music, and playful intimacy insist that pleasure and style are themselves part of political existence.

The film connects sexual regulation to gendered expectations. Kena is pressured to choose a respectable profession and heterosexual future; Ziki’s visible femininity does not protect her from punishment once desire crosses the permitted boundary. Their relationship unsettles both family alliances and the public performance of morality on which male political careers depend. Homophobia functions not as isolated prejudice but as a technology for organizing citizenship.

Rafiki’s production history became part of its meaning. Kenyan authorities banned the film, then briefly allowed screenings so it could qualify for awards consideration. That conflict demonstrated the contradiction at the center of representation: a national cinema can be celebrated internationally while queer citizens are told their stories are not authentically national. Director Wanuri Kahiu’s ‘Afrobubblegum’ aesthetic answers by locating imagination and joy within Africa rather than treating them as imports.

The romance is deliberately accessible, and some structural questions—class difference, long-term organizing, and the uneven risks faced by queer people—remain lightly drawn. But accessibility is not political emptiness. By allowing two young women to be awkward, desirous, hopeful, and visually radiant, the film expands what African feminist and queer cinema can preserve: not only injury, but the futures that repression tries to make unimaginable.
